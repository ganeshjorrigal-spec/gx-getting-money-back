# **Tickback Product Thinking: Research Base for Flight and E-Commerce Refunds in India**

## **1\. Summary**

> 1. Refund disputes in India present a major pain point across the e-commerce and aviation sectors.  
> 2. E-commerce platforms generate the highest volume of consumer grievances and delayed returns.  
> 3. Flight cancellations also cause severe friction due to complex booking agency channels.  
> 4. Current regulations mandate strict compliance timelines for returning consumer funds.  
> 5. Aviation rules require credit card refunds to clear within seven days of cancellation.  
> 6. E-commerce laws require grievance officers to acknowledge complaints within forty eight hours.  
> 7. In practice, companies frequently ignore these rules and trap consumers in support loops.  
> 8. Existing alternatives are either entirely manual, demand upfront fees, or lack domestic jurisdiction.  
> 9. An automated agent creates value by calculating deadlines and citing exact statutory rules.  
> 10. Operating strictly from the user side without handling passwords neutralizes critical security risks.

## **2\. Evidence Base**

### **2.1 Size and Frequency of the Pain**

Published grievance data reveals that the e-commerce sector is the primary driver of refund disputes in India, followed by the travel and aviation sectors. Data from the National Consumer Helpline demonstrates that over an eight month period ending in December 2025, e-commerce accounted for 39,965 grievances resulting in over 32 crore rupees in refunds1. During the same period, travel and tourism recorded 4,050 complaints, while the airline sector recorded 668 complaints1. A shorter two month window in 2025 showed identical sectoral rankings, confirming a consistent trend2.  
In the aviation domain, refunds remain the most persistent issue. A snapshot of the Ministry of Civil Aviation AirSewa portal in October 2026 showed that refunds constituted the absolute majority of all registered airline grievances3. This baseline volume spikes drastically during widespread operational failures. For example, in December 2025, mass flight cancellations by IndiGo paralyzed domestic air travel, leaving millions of passengers stranded and seeking immediate refunds4. The Central Consumer Protection Authority tracks regulatory actions and directs massive refund distributions, such as the 1,454 crore rupees returned for Covid era cancellations, but direct monthly complaint volume data from their proprietary portal is not publicly available6.

### **2.2 The User Journey Today**

The typical refund journey begins with a clear trigger: a flight is cancelled by the airline or an online store order goes undelivered. Upon this trigger, the consumer initiates first contact via the company's automated chat interface or generic customer support email.  
Stalls immediately occur because the rules differ drastically from actual business practices. The rule for aviation, as governed by the Directorate General of Civil Aviation under Civil Aviation Requirements Section 3 Series M Part II, dictates that credit card refunds must be processed by the airline within seven days7. This core regulation was issued in 2008 and updated in 2016, making it older than two years, though secondary travel aggregators confirm its continued active enforcement in 20267. Conversely, the practice is characterized by extreme friction. Secondary sources report that cash refunds stretch to 21 working days, and bookings made via travel agents trigger a deflection loop where the airline and the agent blame each other8.  
For e-commerce, the rule dictates swift resolutions, but the practice involves immense communication friction. Consumer helpline records show users frequently stall on miscommunication regarding order cancellation status and delayed return pickups2. Consequently, users attempt multiple escalation vectors: posting on social media platform X, emailing executives, or calling the National Consumer Helpline. Most consumers abandon their claims before filing formal consumer court paperwork due to the disproportionate time and effort required relative to the refund value.

### **2.3 Where an Agent Adds the Most Value**

An intelligent agent adds maximum value through structured, rule bound escalation. The first step involves knowing the rule and the exact deadline. For online stores, the Consumer Protection E-Commerce Rules 2020 define the regulatory standard. This legislation is older than two years but remains the primary compliance mechanism. The rule mandates that an e-commerce platform's grievance officer must acknowledge a consumer complaint within 48 hours and resolve the issue within one month9.  
The agent adds value by finding the right contact, bypassing Tier 1 support chatbots, and writing the message directly to the designated grievance officer. The agent remembers to chase the company exactly 48 hours after the initial email, escalating in the right order from support, to the Grievance Officer, to the AirSewa portal or National Consumer Helpline. Finally, the agent excels at proving the claim. Airlines routinely attempt to offer credit shells for cancelled flights. The agent cites the specific Civil Aviation Requirement clause stating that holding refund amounts in a credit shell is the prerogative of the passenger, forcing the airline to issue a cash refund7.

### **2.4 Alternatives Users Have Today**

Consumers currently face a fractured landscape of alternatives. Doing nothing is the most common default. Doing it themselves using tools like ChatGPT allows them to draft polite emails, but generative models hallucinate local regulatory clauses and cannot autonomously track the 48 hour follow up deadlines.  
Domestic consumer complaint services exist but carry financial barriers. Voxya, a consumer dispute platform, charges a fixed upfront fee of 2,699 rupees to prepare formal case papers for the consumer forum11. Filing independently through the government's E-daakhil system is cheaper but bureaucratically complex, carrying a statutory court fee of 100 rupees for claims under one lakh rupees12. The National Consumer Helpline operates a toll free 1915 number and acts as an excellent pre-litigation mediator2. However, it relies entirely on the consumer to manually submit evidence and chase the process.  
Global claims agents like AirHelp operate on a contingency fee model but provide no coverage for domestic Indian disputes. AirHelp explicitly states that they do not process compensation claims governed by India's Passenger Charter, restricting their utility to flights touching European jurisdictions under EC 26114.

### **2.5 Risks and Limits**

Deploying an AI agent for grievance redressal introduces legal, operational, and data risks. Legally, the agent must strictly provide factual regulatory information and draft correspondence. Offering predictive legal outcomes crosses into the unauthorized practice of law.  
Data limitations form the core of the product's security architecture. The agent must never collect, log, or process user passwords, one time passwords, or raw bank details. Operating entirely via a case inbox copied on the user's outbound emails mitigates these data risks. Furthermore, navigating e-commerce platform terms of service restricts automated logins. Attempting to log into a user's retail account with automated scripts risks triggering account bans.  
Finally, the agent can make things worse if the underlying playbook is flawed. Citing a wrong deadline, applying a rule to the wrong sector, or directing emails to a defunct nodal officer address will provide the company with valid administrative grounds to dismiss the claim entirely.
