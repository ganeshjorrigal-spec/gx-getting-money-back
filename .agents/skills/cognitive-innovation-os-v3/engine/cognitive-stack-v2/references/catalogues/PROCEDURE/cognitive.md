### Decision Hygiene · #732 · `llm-high` · cognitive · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Kahneman's framework for reducing noise (unwanted variability) in judgment through procedural discipline—the decision-making equivalent of surgical hygiene. Just as surgeons don't rely on individual skill to prevent infection (they follow sterilization protocols), organizations shouldn't rely on individual judgment quality for important decisions—they should follow structured decision protocols. Decision hygiene practices: independent evaluation before group discussion, structured criteria applied systematically, reference class forecasting, and aggregation of multiple independent judgments.
- **How it helps:** Implement decision hygiene for all important, repeatable judgments. The core practices: (1) Before group discussion, have each person evaluate independently (prevents anchoring and groupthink). (2) Use structured evaluation criteria rather than holistic judgment. (3) Use reference classes ('how did similar situations turn out?') rather than inside-view prediction. (4) Aggregate multiple independent judgments rather than relying on one expert. (5) Delay intuitive judgment until structured analysis is complete. These practices don't require special skill—they're procedures anyone can follow.
- **When to use:** When important decisions are made by different people with inconsistent outcomes; when hiring, pricing, diagnosis, or evaluation processes need to be more reliable; when you suspect judgment variability but haven't addressed it structurally; when designing decision processes for organizations.
- **Unique value:** Shifts decision improvement from 'get smarter people' to 'implement better procedures.' Decision hygiene works even with average decision-makers because it addresses the structural sources of noise. The surgical hygiene analogy is precise: individual brilliance doesn't prevent infection—protocol does. Individual brilliance doesn't prevent noise—decision hygiene does.
- **Limits:** Structured procedures can feel bureaucratic and slow. Some decisions genuinely benefit from holistic, intuitive judgment. Over-structuring can prevent novel insights that don't fit the template. Decision hygiene reduces noise but doesn't address bias. Implementation requires organizational discipline that's hard to maintain.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein's noise research (2021) and behavioral economics' debiasing literature. Draws on statistical decision theory, clinical prediction rules research, and organizational behavior on structured analytic procedures. Opposes ad hoc intuitive judgment.

### Decision Fatigue Reduction Strategies · #1433 · `llm-high` · cognitive · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Decision fatigue is the deterioration of decision quality after a long session of decision-making, documented in Shai Danziger's famous study showing that Israeli judges granted parole at 65% in morning sessions but dropped to nearly 0% just before breaks, rebounding to 65% after eating. Each decision — no matter how trivial — consumes cognitive resources from the same pool used for consequential decisions. The implication is stark: the person who chooses between 50 email responses before a strategic meeting is making that strategic decision with a depleted cognitive budget. Reduction strategies include: (1) Decision elimination (automate or standardize routine choices — Obama's gray suits, Zuckerberg's gray t-shirts), (2) Decision batching (group similar decisions into a single session), (3) Decision scheduling (place high-stakes decisions in your peak cognitive window), (4) Decision precommitment (make decisions in advance when you're fresh — tomorrow's clothes, weekly meal plans, pre-set if-then rules for routine situations), and (5) Decision delegation (identify decisions where your input doesn't meaningfully improve the outcome and delegate them).
- **How it helps:** Knowledge workers make an estimated 35,000 decisions per day, most of them trivial. The intervention is to reduce that number dramatically. Audit your daily decisions: which ones don't meaningfully improve with your personal judgment? Automate those (subscription services, standing orders, template responses, default calendar blocks). Which ones could be made in advance? Batch those into a weekly planning session. Which ones are high-stakes? Protect those by scheduling them first and arriving fresh. A CEO who eliminates wardrobe decisions, lunch decisions, and commute route decisions saves cognitive resources for decisions that actually matter.
- **When to use:** When end-of-day decisions are consistently worse than morning decisions. When trivial choices consume disproportionate mental energy. When designing personal systems for productivity. When decision quality matters and you want to maximize it by reducing quantity.
- **Unique value:** Provides specific, actionable strategies for reducing the number of decisions rather than the vague advice to 'make better decisions.' The five reduction strategies (eliminate, batch, schedule, precommit, delegate) each address a different type of unnecessary decision load.
- **Limits:** Not all decisions can be eliminated or automated without losing beneficial variation. Over-automating life can create rigidity and reduce serendipity. The Danziger study has been partially critiqued (hunger effects may explain more than decision fatigue). Some people enjoy making decisions and find standardization stifling.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Roy Baumeister's ego depletion research and decision science (Schwartz's paradox of choice). Draws on behavioral economics, cognitive load theory (Sweller), and habit formation research from dual-process psychology (Kahneman's System 1/2 framework).

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy Skills Framework · #2044 · `llm-high` · cognitive · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), created by Marsha Linehan, teaches four skill modules designed to address the core deficits in emotion regulation that drive self-destructive behavior. Mindfulness: observing and describing internal experience without judgment, creating space between stimulus and response. Distress Tolerance: surviving crisis moments without making things worse — radical acceptance, self-soothing, distraction, and TIPP (Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Progressive relaxation). Emotion Regulation: understanding emotions, reducing vulnerability to emotional reactivity (PLEASE skills: Physical health, eating, avoiding substances, Sleep, Exercise), and building positive experiences. Interpersonal Effectiveness: asserting needs while maintaining relationships — DEAR MAN (Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce, Mindful, Appear confident, Negotiate).
- **How it helps:** DBT skills are increasingly taught outside of clinical populations because they address universal human challenges: managing overwhelming emotions, surviving crises without self-destructive coping, and communicating needs assertively. In high-stress professional environments, distress tolerance skills prevent reactive decision-making during crises. The DEAR MAN framework for difficult conversations is more specific and actionable than generic 'communication skills' advice. The dialectical stance — fully accepting the current situation while actively working to change it — resolves the common paralysis between 'accept what you can't change' and 'never give up.' In leadership, modeling this.
- **When to use:** When emotions are overwhelming rational decision-making, during personal or professional crises that tempt self-destructive responses, when you need to assert needs without damaging relationships, or when you're stuck between acceptance and change and need a framework for holding both simultaneously.
- **Unique value:** Provides four specific, structured skill sets (not just concepts) for managing the emotional challenges that undermine effectiveness. The dialectical framework — acceptance AND change — resolves a false dichotomy that traps many people. The evidence base for DBT is among the strongest in psychotherapy.
- **Limits:** DBT was designed for borderline personality disorder and may be overly structured for people with milder emotional regulation challenges. Skill acquisition requires extensive practice, not just intellectual understanding. The full DBT program (individual therapy + skills group + phone coaching + consultation team) is resource-intensive.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Linehan's biosocial theory of borderline personality, dialectical philosophy (Hegel's thesis-antithesis-synthesis), and Zen mindfulness traditions. Draws on cognitive behavioral therapy, behavior analysis, acceptance and commitment therapy, and neuroscience research on emotion regulation and distress tolerance.

### Annual Review Decision Audit · #2281 · `high` · cognitive · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A structured year-end protocol for evaluating the quality of your major decisions over the past 12 months, designed to build decision-making skill over time. The protocol: (1) List every significant decision you made during the year (career, financial, relationship, health, projects). (2) For each decision, record: the outcome (positive/negative/neutral), the reasoning you used at the time, what you knew then vs what you know now, and whether the outcome was due to good/bad decision process or good/bad luck. (3) Classify each decision into four quadrants: Good Process + Good Outcome (deserved success — repeat this), Good Process + Bad Outcome (bad luck — don't change your process), Bad Process + Good Outcome (undeserved success — don't get overconfident), Bad Process + Bad Outcome (deserved failure — learn and adjust). (4) Identify your three best and three worst decisions — what patterns do you see? (5) Set 2-3 decision-making intentions for next year.
- **How it helps:** Installs the most important meta-skill in decision-making: separating decision quality from outcome quality. Most people reinforce bad decisions that happened to work out and abandon good decisions that happened to fail — this audit breaks that cycle. The four-quadrant classification is immediately revelatory. After 2-3 annual audits, most people report significantly improved self-awareness about their decision-making patterns and biases.
- **When to use:** Annually, during a dedicated reflection period (year-end, birthday, or personal new year). After major life transitions to assess the decisions that led there. When you notice a pattern of similar mistakes. Can also be done quarterly at a lighter level.
- **Unique value:** The only review protocol that explicitly separates luck from skill in decision outcomes. This distinction — which professional poker players and investors use intuitively — is absent from most personal reflection practices and is the single biggest upgrade to learning from experience.
- **Limits:** Hindsight bias makes it very difficult to honestly assess what you knew at the time of the decision. The luck/skill distinction is often genuinely ambiguous. Requires accurate memory or records of past reasoning (decision journals help). Can become self-critical rumination if not framed constructively.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in metacognition research and Kahneman's work on hindsight bias. Draws on Tetlock's superforecasting research — calibration requires comparing predictions to outcomes. Reflects the organizational learning literature (Argyris, Schon) on double-loop learning: examining assumptions, not just outcomes.
