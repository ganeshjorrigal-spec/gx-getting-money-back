### Progressive Assembly Sequence · #2129 · `high` · design · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Experienced builders understand that assembly order is often as important as component quality. Progressive assembly sequences operations so each step creates reference surfaces, access points, or structural support for subsequent steps—and later steps don't require undoing earlier ones. A cabinet maker glues the carcase first, then installs shelves (referencing the carcase), then fits doors, then finishes. In construction, the sequence foundation-framing-roofing-mechanicals-insulation-drywall-finish exists because each trade needs the previous work as substrate and access subsequent trades would block. The most expensive mistakes are sequencing errors—drywall before the electrician finishes means cutting open finished walls.
- **How it helps:** Progressive assembly thinking applies to any multi-step process where sequence matters. In software, deploying the database schema before the application code that depends on it. In project management, completing foundational decisions before detail decisions that depend on them. In organizational design, establishing structure before processes, and processes before performance metrics. The model teaches that sequencing errors are among the most expensive mistakes in any domain because they require rework that destroys completed work. The key diagnostic question is: 'Does this step require anything that hasn't been completed yet, and will it block anything that needs to happen later?'
- **When to use:** When planning any multi-step project where the order of operations matters. When troubleshooting projects that have stalled or required extensive rework—sequencing errors are often the root cause. When designing workflows that must be followed by different people or teams working in series.
- **Unique value:** Makes explicit a form of tacit knowledge that experienced project managers develop intuitively: the critical importance of operation sequencing. Provides specific diagnostic questions for evaluating whether a proposed sequence will create access, reference, or substrate problems at later stages.
- **Limits:** Optimal sequencing may be computationally intractable in complex projects with many interdependencies. Rigid sequencing reduces flexibility to adapt to changes. Some 'wrong' sequences can be accommodated with workarounds cheaper than optimal sequencing. Parallel work streams sometimes require accepting suboptimal sequencing for time savings.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Polanyi's tacit knowledge epistemology, phenomenology of skilled practice (Merleau-Ponty, Dreyfus), and materials science. Draws on cognitive science of expertise (Ericsson), embodied cognition research, and philosophy of technology. Influenced by Sennett's craft theory, Crawford's manual work philosophy, and neuroscience of sensorimotor integration.
