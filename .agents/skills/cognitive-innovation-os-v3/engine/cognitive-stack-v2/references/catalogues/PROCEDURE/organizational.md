### Baseball Card System (for People) · #299 · `llm-high` · organizational · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Ray Dalio's Bridgewater practice of creating comprehensive profiles for each person—'baseball cards'—that document their strengths, weaknesses, reliability in specific domains, and track record of predictions and decisions. Like a baseball card showing a player's batting average, ERA, and fielding percentage, the people baseball card shows demonstrated capability across multiple dimensions. The system enables believability-weighted decision-making: when a strategic decision arises, you can identify who has the strongest track record in THAT specific domain and weight their input accordingly. The baseball card is living data, not fixed labels—it updates continuously based on observed performance.
- **How it helps:** Build informal baseball cards for your key relationships. For each person: what are they demonstrably excellent at? Where have they been consistently wrong? What's their track record on the specific type of decision at hand? The practice: track predictions and assessments explicitly—who said what would happen, and what actually happened? Over time, this produces calibrated assessments of who to trust on which topics. For organizations: even without Bridgewater's formal system, maintaining mental models of people's proven strengths by domain dramatically improves team composition and decision-weighting.
- **When to use:** When assembling teams for specific challenges and need to match strengths to requirements; when decision-weighting should reflect demonstrated competence rather than hierarchy; when performance evaluation needs to be domain-specific rather than holistic; when building a culture of meritocratic believability weighting.
- **Unique value:** Converts subjective impressions of people into domain-specific, evidence-based capability profiles. The key innovation: capabilities are tracked BY DOMAIN, not globally—someone excellent at strategy may be poor at execution, and the baseball card captures both. This prevents the halo effect from corrupting capability assessment.
- **Limits:** Formal tracking systems can feel surveillance-like and erode trust. People can change, making historical data misleading. Some capabilities resist quantification. The system requires psychological safety that many cultures lack. Gaming the metrics is always a risk.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Ray Dalio's organizational management philosophy and knowledge management theory. Draws on CRM (Customer Relationship Management) methodology, social network analysis, and the art of building personal intelligence files. Connected to intelligence tradecraft and reputation systems research.

### Five-Step Process (Goals → Problems → Diagnosis → Design → Execution) · #306 · `high` · organizational · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Ray Dalio's iterative framework for systematic progress: (1) Set clear GOALS. (2) Identify PROBLEMS that stand in the way. (3) DIAGNOSE problems to their root causes. (4) DESIGN solutions that address root causes. (5) EXECUTE the solutions. Then cycle back to step 1. The framework's power is in the sequencing: most people jump from problems to solutions (step 2 to step 5), skipping the critical diagnosis and design steps. Without diagnosis, you treat symptoms rather than causes. Without design, you implement the first solution rather than the best one. The five-step process forces intellectual honesty at each stage.
- **How it helps:** Apply the sequence explicitly to any significant challenge. The most critical discipline: do NOT skip from problem identification (step 2) to execution (step 5). Force yourself through diagnosis ('what's the root cause?') and design ('what's the best solution to the root cause?') before acting. The common failure modes: (1) Fuzzy goals (step 1 weakness—you can't identify problems if you don't know what you're trying to achieve). (2) Problem denial (step 2 weakness—not acknowledging problems). (3) Shallow diagnosis (step 3 weakness—treating symptoms). (4) No design phase (step 4 weakness—executing the first idea).
- **When to use:** When problem-solving efforts keep addressing symptoms without reaching root causes; when execution happens without adequate design; when goals are unclear and problems are poorly defined; when a systematic framework would improve the quality of any improvement effort.
- **Unique value:** The framework's value is in what it PREVENTS: the skip from problem to execution that produces symptom-treating rather than cause-addressing. By making diagnosis and design explicit required steps, the five-step process forces the intellectual work that most people and organizations unconsciously bypass.
- **Limits:** The linear sequence is idealized—real progress is messier. Some problems require immediate action before diagnosis is complete. Over-diagnosing can produce analysis paralysis. The framework assumes clear goals, which aren't always available. Some problems don't have root causes that can be diagnosed.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Ray Dalio drawing on management by objectives (Drucker), systems thinking, and iterative process methodology. Roots in cybernetic feedback loops (Wiener), goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham), and PDCA cycles (Deming). Connected to OKR frameworks and strategic planning methodology.

### Stand-Up Meeting Protocol (Daily Sync) · #2299 · `high` · organizational · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A rapid daily alignment protocol limiting each participant to three statements, completed standing up to enforce brevity. Each person answers three questions in under two minutes: (1) What did I accomplish yesterday? (2) What will I work on today? (3) What's blocking my progress? The protocol rules: Stand up (physical constraint on duration). Time-box to 15 minutes maximum regardless of team size (for teams over 8, split into sub-groups). No problem-solving during standup — blockers are noted and taken offline immediately after. Same time, same place, every day. The facilitator's job is to interrupt tangents with: 'Let's take that offline.' If the meeting consistently runs over 15 minutes, the team is either too large or using the standup for the wrong purpose.
- **How it helps:** Eliminates the need for most status-update meetings while surfacing blockers within 24 hours of their emergence. Teams using daily standups report 25-40% reduction in total meeting time because the standup prevents the 'quick sync' meetings that proliferate otherwise. The physical standing constraint is surprisingly effective — sitting meetings naturally expand to fill available time. Immediately implementable with any team, no tools required.
- **When to use:** Daily, with any team working on shared deliverables. Particularly valuable for project teams, product development, cross-functional groups, and any environment where coordination delays are costly. Can be virtual (with cameras on and strict time limits). Use during intense project phases and consider reducing frequency during maintenance phases.
- **Unique value:** The only meeting format that uses physical constraint (standing) as a design element to enforce brevity. The three-question structure makes it impossible to ramble or use the meeting for problem-solving — both of which plague every other meeting format.
- **Limits:** Can become performative ('reciting yesterday's ticket numbers') if the team doesn't value the ritual. Accessibility concerns for people who cannot stand. Time zones make daily standups difficult for distributed teams. The format doesn't scale beyond 12 people without modification. Can feel like surveillance if the culture isn't psychologically safe.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in agile methodology (Schwaber & Sutherland, Scrum framework) and organizational behavior research on coordination costs. Draws on information-processing theory in organizations — brief structured synchronization reduces communication overhead. Reflects research on team performance showing shared situational awareness is a primary coordination mechanism.

### After-Action Review Protocol (AAR) · #2310 · `high` · organizational · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A structured team learning protocol developed by the U.S. Army and adapted for business contexts, conducted immediately after any significant event, project, or milestone. The protocol follows four questions in strict order: (1) What did we intend to happen? — Establish the original plan, objectives, and expected outcomes. This must be stated FIRST, before outcomes are discussed, to prevent hindsight bias from rewriting intentions. (2) What actually happened? — Document the actual outcomes factually. What went right? What went wrong? What was unexpected? (3) Why was there a difference? — Analyze root causes of the gap between intention and reality. Distinguish between factors within the team's control and external factors. (4) What will we do differently next time? — Generate specific, actionable changes. Assign owners and timelines. The AAR should be conducted within 24-48 hours of the event, involve all key participants, be blame-free, and be documented. Critical rule: AARs are learning exercises, not performance evaluations.
- **How it helps:** The most proven organizational learning protocol in existence, battle-tested across military and business contexts for decades. The strict separation of 'intended vs actual' prevents the revisionist history that plagues most post-project reviews ('We never expected it to go perfectly'). The 24-48 hour window captures lessons while memory is accurate. Organizations that conduct AARs consistently report significantly fewer repeated mistakes and faster adaptation to changing conditions.
- **When to use:** After any significant event: project completions, product launches, incidents, crises, organizational changes, failed initiatives, and notable successes. After negotiations, presentations, or sales pitches. Weekly during long-running projects. The lower the threshold for triggering an AAR, the faster the team learns.
- **Unique value:** The only learning protocol that begins by documenting intentions BEFORE discussing outcomes. This seemingly simple structural choice eliminates hindsight bias — the cognitive distortion that makes every review less useful by rewriting what people 'expected' to match what actually happened.
- **Limits:** Requires psychological safety — in blame-oriented cultures, people will not share honestly. The 'no-blame' rule can be hard to maintain when failures had real consequences. Action items from AARs often compete with ongoing work for attention. Requires discipline to conduct consistently, especially after successes (when learning motivation is lowest).
- **Foundations:** Developed from U.S. Army Center for Army Lessons Learned. Rooted in organizational learning theory (Argyris, Schon) and action research. The AAR format reflects research on feedback timing — immediate post-event review captures tacit knowledge before it fades. Draws on deliberate practice theory (Ericsson) applied to team performance.
