### SCAMPER Method · #153 · `high` · creativity · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A structured creativity technique using seven prompts to systematically generate innovations from existing products, processes, or ideas: Substitute (what can replace a component?), Combine (what can merge?), Adapt (what can be adjusted from another context?), Modify/Magnify (what can be changed in scale or attribute?), Put to another use (what else could this serve?), Eliminate (what can be removed?), Reverse/Rearrange (what can be reordered or inverted?). SCAMPER works because it forces systematic exploration of the modification space rather than waiting for inspiration.
- **How it helps:** When innovation feels stuck, run any product, process, or strategy through all seven SCAMPER prompts. Each prompt opens a different dimension of the solution space. Eliminate is often the most powerful and least used: what happens if we remove this feature, this step, this requirement? Combine is the innovation engine behind most consumer products: the smartphone combined phone + camera + music player + GPS + internet. The method works because creative blocks usually result from exploring only one or two modification dimensions.
- **When to use:** When brainstorming needs structure to avoid circling the same ideas; when an existing product or process needs refreshing; when innovation efforts keep generating incremental rather than divergent ideas; when a team is stuck and needs a systematic way to explore the solution space.
- **Unique value:** Converts creativity from waiting for lightning to strike into a systematic process. Each SCAMPER prompt opens a specific dimension of innovation that the others miss. Running through all seven guarantees broader exploration than unstructured brainstorming, while remaining simple enough for anyone to facilitate.
- **Limits:** SCAMPER generates modifications of existing things, not entirely new categories. It can produce a long list of ideas without clear criteria for evaluation. Some prompts are more productive than others depending on the domain. The method works best for product and process innovation, less well for strategic or paradigmatic innovation.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Bob Eberle building on Osborn's brainstorming methodology; rooted in applied creativity research and cognitive heuristics. Connects to morphological analysis in design theory, analogical reasoning research, and combinatorial innovation in economics.
- **Recognition cue:** "For each letter, what would that move produce here?"
- **Worked example:** Designing a new product feature — run through SCAMPER to force seven distinct generation moves rather than brainstorming generically

### Brainstorming Rules Protocol (IDEO Method) · #2301 · `high` · creativity · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A structured brainstorming protocol based on IDEO's design thinking practice, with specific rules that make the difference between productive ideation and the unfocused sessions most teams experience. The seven rules, posted visibly during every session: (1) Defer judgment — no evaluating ideas during generation. (2) Encourage wild ideas — the craziest idea often sparks the best one. (3) Build on the ideas of others — say 'Yes, and...' not 'No, but...' (4) Stay focused on the topic — one conversation at a time. (5) One person speaks at a time. (6) Be visual — sketch ideas, don't just describe them. (7) Go for quantity — aim for 100 ideas in 60 minutes. The session structure: (a) Frame the problem as a 'How might we...?' question. (b) Silent individual brainstorm first (5 min). (c) Share and build (30-45 min). (d) Cluster and vote (15 min). (e) Select top 3-5 ideas for development. Critical addition: the silent individual phase prevents anchoring on the first idea and ensures introverts contribute equally.
- **How it helps:** Solves the well-documented failure of traditional brainstorming (which research shows produces fewer and worse ideas than individuals working alone) by adding the silent-first phase and explicit rules. The 'How might we' framing is surprisingly powerful — it prevents premature problem-solving and opens up solution space. The quantity target (100 ideas) pushes past the obvious solutions that everyone already thought of into genuinely novel territory. Immediately implementable with any team.
- **When to use:** When a team needs to generate novel solutions to a defined problem. Product feature ideation, service design, marketing campaigns, process improvement, event planning. NOT useful for problems that have a single correct answer or require expert analysis. Best when the problem is well-defined but the solution space is wide open.
- **Unique value:** The only brainstorming protocol that explicitly addresses the research finding that unstructured brainstorming is worse than individual ideation. The silent-first phase and quantity target are the key innovations that make group brainstorming actually outperform solo thinking.
- **Limits:** Requires a facilitator strong enough to enforce the 'defer judgment' rule — one critical comment can shut down an entire session. Doesn't work well remotely without specialized tools. The 100-idea target can feel forced for some teams. Ideas generated still need rigorous evaluation afterward — brainstorming generates quantity, not quality.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in IDEO's design thinking methodology and Osborn's original brainstorming research. Draws on social psychology of creativity (Diehl & Stroebe) — deferred judgment increases idea generation. Reflects cognitive psychology research on associative thinking and the research on quantity-quality relationships in ideation.
