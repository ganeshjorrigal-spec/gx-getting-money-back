### Nonviolent Communication (Rosenberg): Observation, Feeling, Need, Request · #133 · `llm-high` · communication · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Marshall Rosenberg's communication framework designed to express needs and resolve conflicts without blame, criticism, or coercion. The four-step structure: (1) Observation: state what happened factually without evaluation ('When you arrived 30 minutes after we agreed'—not 'when you were late again'). (2) Feeling: name the emotion it triggered ('I felt frustrated'). (3) Need: identify the underlying need ('because I need reliability in our plans'). (4) Request: make a specific, actionable, positive request ('would you be willing to text me if you're going to be more than 10 minutes late?'). NVC's power comes from separating OBSERVATION from EVALUATION and connecting FEELINGS to NEEDS rather than to blame.
- **How it helps:** Practice each step separately before combining them. The most difficult steps: (1) Observation without evaluation—'you're always late' is evaluation; 'you arrived at 7:30 when we agreed on 7:00' is observation. (2) Connecting feelings to needs rather than to the other person's actions—'I feel frustrated because I need reliability' rather than 'you make me frustrated.' The complete formula: 'When [observation], I feel [feeling], because I need [need]. Would you be willing to [request]?' For leaders: NVC transforms feedback from accusation to connection—addressing the issue without attacking the person.
- **When to use:** When communication defaults to blame, criticism, or demand; when conflicts need de-escalation through needs-based rather than blame-based dialogue; when feedback needs to address behavior without attacking character; when the connection between feelings and underlying needs would transform defensive conversations into collaborative ones.
- **Unique value:** Provides the specific linguistic structure that converts blame into connection: by routing communication through NEEDS rather than JUDGMENTS, NVC transforms adversarial exchanges into collaborative problem-solving. The observation/evaluation distinction alone eliminates most conversational defensiveness.
- **Limits:** NVC can feel formulaic and unnatural when first practiced. Some situations require directness that NVC's structure may slow down. The framework assumes both parties are willing to engage. Cultural context affects whether NVC's style is appropriate. Some critics argue NVC can be used manipulatively.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in humanistic psychology (Rogers' person-centered therapy) and needs theory (Maslow). Draws on conflict resolution research, empathic listening traditions, and Gandhi's nonviolent philosophy adapted to interpersonal communication.

### Active Listening Protocol (Reflective Listening Steps) · #2272 · `high` · communication · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A structured five-step protocol for listening that creates genuine understanding rather than waiting-to-respond. Step 1 — Attend: Give full physical attention — face the speaker, maintain eye contact, put away devices, lean in slightly. Step 2 — Receive: Listen for content AND emotion. Notice what's said and what's avoided. Don't formulate your response while they're speaking. Step 3 — Reflect: Paraphrase what you heard ('So what I'm hearing is...') and name the emotion you sense ('...and it sounds like that was really frustrating'). Step 4 — Confirm: Ask 'Did I get that right?' and adjust until they say yes. Step 5 — Respond: Only after confirming understanding, share your perspective or ask your next question. The discipline: count to three after they stop speaking before you respond — most people aren't done when they first pause. Ask at least two follow-up questions before offering any opinion.
- **How it helps:** Transforms conversations from parallel monologues into genuine dialogue. Research shows that most people listen to reply, not to understand — this protocol reverses that default. The 'count to three' rule alone will improve every conversation you have. The reflect-and-confirm cycle ensures you're responding to what was actually communicated, not what you assumed. Immediately deployable in your next conversation with zero preparation.
- **When to use:** Every important conversation, but especially: when someone is upset, when you disagree, when the topic is emotionally charged, when you're in a position of authority, when trying to understand a perspective very different from your own, and when you notice yourself mentally rehearsing your rebuttal.
- **Unique value:** While 'active listening' is widely taught as a concept, this protocol specifies the exact behavioral sequence. The 'confirm before responding' step creates a structural guarantee that the speaker feels heard before the conversation moves on — something that unstructured 'good listening' rarely achieves.
- **Limits:** Can feel performative if the listener isn't genuinely interested. The reflect-and-confirm cycle can slow fast-paced conversations to the point of frustration. Not appropriate for all contexts — casual social conversation doesn't need this level of structure. Can be used manipulatively to create false rapport.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Carl Rogers' person-centered therapy and his concept of empathic understanding. Draws on communication theory, interpersonal neurobiology (Siegel), and the transactional model of communication. Research by Goleman on emotional intelligence shows reflective listening increases perceived understanding and reduces defensive communication.
