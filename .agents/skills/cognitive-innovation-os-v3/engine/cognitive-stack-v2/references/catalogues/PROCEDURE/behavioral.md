### Dual Process Model of Grief (Stroebe & Schut) — Applied · #1458 · `high` · behavioral · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** The Dual Process Model (DPM) proposes that healthy grieving involves oscillation between two orientations: loss-oriented coping (confronting the grief itself—crying, yearning, dwelling on the deceased) and restoration-oriented coping (attending to life changes that result from loss—learning new skills, forming new identities, managing daily tasks). The critical insight is that neither orientation alone constitutes healthy grief. People who only do loss-oriented processing become stuck in chronic grief; those who only do restoration work may experience delayed grief complications. The oscillation between the two—sometimes within the same hour—is the adaptive mechanism. This model also explains gender differences: many men lean toward restoration orientation (practical tasks), which others may misread as 'not grieving properly.'
- **How it helps:** This model is exceptionally useful for knowledge workers navigating major transitions because it validates both emotional processing AND practical forward motion. After a job loss, you might spend the morning updating your LinkedIn (restoration) and the afternoon mourning your lost team (loss-orientation)—the DPM says this oscillation IS the healthy response. Use it to: (1) stop judging yourself for 'not being productive' during grief or for 'avoiding your feelings' when you're being practical, (2) understand why some team members seem to 'move on too fast' while others seem stuck, (3) design reintegration timelines after organizational loss.
- **When to use:** When grieving individuals (including yourself) seem to swing between deep sadness and productive functioning and you're unsure if this is healthy. When designing organizational support after layoffs or major changes. When you notice someone is stuck exclusively in one orientation.
- **Unique value:** Replaces the idea that grief is a linear progression with a dynamic oscillation model backed by strong empirical evidence. Uniquely explains why distraction and avoidance can be adaptive, not pathological. The only major grief model that explicitly accounts for the practical life-rebuilding dimension.
- **Limits:** Developed primarily in Western bereavement contexts. May not fully capture grief in collectivist cultures where communal processing dominates. The oscillation pattern may not apply equally to traumatic or violent loss. Doesn't specify optimal ratios of loss vs. restoration orientation.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Stroebe and Schut's oscillation theory from grief psychology, integrating coping theory (Lazarus) and attachment theory. Draws on bereavement research distinguishing loss-orientation from restoration-orientation as adaptive oscillating processes.

### Emotional Regulation Toolkit (DBT-Informed TIPP Protocol) · #2311 · `high` · behavioral · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A four-technique emergency protocol from Dialectical Behavior Therapy for rapidly reducing emotional intensity when you're in crisis or emotional flooding. TIPP: T — Temperature: Apply cold water or an ice pack to your face (especially forehead and cheeks) for 30 seconds. This activates the dive reflex, immediately lowering heart rate and calming the nervous system. I — Intense Exercise: Do 20-30 minutes of vigorous exercise (running, jumping jacks, pushups) to metabolize stress hormones. Even 5 minutes helps. P — Paced Breathing: Exhale longer than you inhale (4-count inhale, 7-count exhale). Extended exhales activate the parasympathetic nervous system. Do for 2-5 minutes. P — Progressive Muscle Relaxation: Starting from your feet, tense each muscle group for 5 seconds, then release. Work up to your face. The full cycle takes 10-15 minutes. Use these techniques IN ORDER of urgency: Temperature for crisis-level emotion, then exercise, then breathing, then relaxation. Each technique takes the edge off enough to access the next one.
- **How it helps:** Provides the fastest evidence-based methods for reducing emotional intensity when thinking-based strategies are impossible (because the prefrontal cortex is offline during emotional flooding). The temperature technique alone can reduce heart rate by 10-25 BPM in 30 seconds — faster than any other technique. These are not coping strategies; they are physiological interventions that work whether or not you believe in them. Immediately actionable: the next time you feel overwhelmed, splash cold water on your face and notice the immediate shift.
- **When to use:** When emotions are at 8/10 or above and you cannot think clearly. During panic attacks, rage episodes, intense grief waves, or overwhelming anxiety. Before making any decision while emotionally flooded. When you need to calm down FAST to function in a meeting, conversation, or crisis.
- **Unique value:** The only emotional regulation protocol that works through physiology rather than cognition. All other regulation techniques (reframing, mindfulness, journaling) require the thinking brain to be online. TIPP works even when thinking is impossible — it calms the body first, which then allows the mind to come back online.
- **Limits:** These are emergency tools, not long-term solutions — they manage the crisis but don't address root causes. Cold water immersion may not be practical in all settings. Intense exercise requires physical ability and appropriate environment. Doesn't replace therapy for chronic emotional dysregulation.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Dialectical Behavior Therapy (Linehan, 1993), developed for borderline personality disorder and extended broadly. TIPP draws on neuroscience of arousal regulation — temperature, exercise, and breathing directly modulate the autonomic nervous system. Reflects behavioral medicine research on physiological intervention in emotional dysregulation.

### Habit Stacking Protocol (James Clear) · #2312 · `high` · behavioral · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A behavior design protocol for building new habits by linking them to existing habits, leveraging the brain's existing neural pathways. The formula: 'After I [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT].' Step 1 — List your existing daily habits (brush teeth, pour coffee, sit at desk, eat lunch, walk through door after work). Step 2 — Match the new habit you want to build with the existing habit that best fits in terms of timing, location, and energy level. Step 3 — Make the new habit tiny: the initial version should take less than 2 minutes ('After I pour my coffee, I will write one sentence in my journal'). Step 4 — Execute the stack consistently for 2-3 weeks before expanding. Step 5 — Chain multiple new habits together once each link is established: 'After I pour coffee → journal one sentence → review today's priorities → do 5 pushups.' Advanced technique: Create habit stacks for different parts of your day — morning stack, work-arrival stack, post-lunch stack, evening stack. The existing habit serves as the cue, eliminating the need for reminders, willpower, or motivation.
- **How it helps:** Solves the #1 reason new habits fail: there's no reliable trigger. Motivation fades, reminders get ignored, and willpower depletes. An existing habit provides a built-in, automatic trigger that fires every day without effort. The 2-minute rule prevents the overwhelm that kills most habit attempts. Success rate for habit stacking is dramatically higher than for habits that rely on time-based triggers ('I'll meditate at 7 AM') because existing habits are more reliable cues than clock times.
- **When to use:** When you want to build any new daily habit. When previous habit attempts have failed due to forgetting or lack of motivation. When designing a morning routine, evening routine, or work routine. When you want to build multiple habits simultaneously by chaining them.
- **Unique value:** The only habit formation protocol that eliminates the need for external reminders or internal motivation. By piggybacking on existing neural pathways, it makes habit initiation automatic rather than effortful — which is the defining characteristic of habits.
- **Limits:** Requires a stable existing habit to anchor to — chaotic schedules undermine the approach. The 2-minute starter may feel too small for ambitious goals, leading people to skip the tiny version. Habit chains can break if one link fails. Doesn't address the deeper motivational and identity-level changes that sustain long-term behavior change.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in James Clear's synthesis of behavior change research and habit formation science. Draws on Fogg's behavior model and Duhigg's habit loop (cue-routine-reward). Habit stacking exploits existing neural pathways — attaching new behaviors to established habits reduces the implementation intention burden.
