### Redundancy in Communication · #297 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The deliberate use of multiple channels, repetitions, and overlapping messages to ensure critical information survives transmission through noisy environments. Natural languages are approximately 50% redundant—which is why you can read misspelled words and understand accented speech. Shannon proved that redundancy is the solution to noisy channels: by saying the same thing in different ways, through different channels, at different times, you ensure that the essential information gets through even when any single transmission fails.
- **How it helps:** For critical information, build in structured redundancy: communicate through multiple channels (written + verbal + visual), repeat key messages across time (not just once), and use different framings for the same core idea. The common complaint 'I already sent an email about that' reveals a misunderstanding of communication physics: one message through one channel in a noisy environment has a high probability of being lost. Important messages need 5-7 exposures through multiple channels before they're reliably received. This isn't inefficiency—it's engineering for reliability.
- **When to use:** When critical messages aren't reaching their audience despite being 'sent'; when organizational alignment requires shared understanding; when designing communication plans for important changes; when the cost of a message being missed exceeds the cost of repeating it.
- **Unique value:** Applies information theory's core insight to organizational communication: reliability requires redundancy. This reframes 'over-communication' as engineering prudence and 'I already told them' as a fundamental misunderstanding of how noisy channels work.
- **Limits:** Excessive redundancy becomes noise itself—at some point, repeated messages are tuned out. The optimal amount of redundancy depends on channel noise, which varies. Redundancy is costly (time, attention). Different messages need different levels of redundancy—not everything warrants multi-channel repetition.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Shannon's information theory and communications engineering. Draws on Hamming's error detection work, the mathematics of redundancy and entropy, and evolutionary biology (genetic redundancy). Connected to organizational resilience research, backup systems engineering, and robustness theory.

### Behavioral Models-Model · #377 · `high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Scott Page's meta-framework for incorporating behavioral science into formal models: instead of assuming perfect rationality, behavioral models include specific psychological tendencies (loss aversion, present bias, social preferences, bounded rationality) as model parameters. The key insight: behavioral deviations from rationality aren't random—they're SYSTEMATIC and PREDICTABLE, which means they can be modeled. Prospect theory replaces expected utility with value functions that include loss aversion. Hyperbolic discounting replaces exponential discounting. Social preference models include fairness, reciprocity, and status concerns alongside material payoffs.
- **How it helps:** When rational actor predictions fail systematically: incorporate the specific behavioral tendency that explains the deviation. The approach: (1) Start with rational prediction. (2) Identify systematic deviation. (3) Incorporate the relevant behavioral parameter (loss aversion, present bias, social proof). (4) Generate improved prediction. For business: price changes that trigger loss aversion (price increases) need different framing than those that don't (new feature pricing). For negotiation: incorporate fairness preferences—people reject 'unfair' offers even when acceptance would be rational.
- **When to use:** When rational models systematically mis-predict behavior; when specific behavioral tendencies need to be incorporated into analysis; when the gap between rational prediction and actual behavior is systematic and identifiable; when formal models need behavioral realism.
- **Unique value:** Bridges the gap between formal modeling (precise but unrealistic) and behavioral science (realistic but imprecise) by incorporating specific behavioral parameters into formal model structures. This produces models that are both rigorous AND behaviorally realistic.
- **Limits:** Behavioral parameters vary across individuals and contexts. The number of potential behavioral modifications is large. Incorporating too many behavioral parameters makes models unwieldy. Which behavioral tendency matters most in each context requires judgment.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in behavioral economics (Kahneman & Tversky), bounded rationality theory (Simon), and agent-based modeling. Draws on cognitive psychology's heuristics-and-biases tradition, experimental economics, and social simulation. Contrasts with rational actor models by incorporating systematic cognitive deviations.

### Hidden Action and Hidden Information · #405 · `high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Two fundamental information problems in economics: HIDDEN ACTION (moral hazard)—one party's behavior can't be observed by the other after a contract is formed (an insured driver drives less carefully; a hired manager shirks). HIDDEN INFORMATION (adverse selection)—one party has information the other lacks before a contract is formed (a seller knows the car is a lemon; an insurance applicant knows they're high-risk). Both problems arise from INFORMATION ASYMMETRY and can cause markets to fail: adverse selection can drive good products out of markets (Akerlof's lemons problem); moral hazard can make incentive contracts ineffective.
- **How it helps:** Design contracts and institutions to mitigate both problems. For hidden action (moral hazard): create monitoring systems, align incentives through equity or performance pay, or design self-revealing mechanisms. For hidden information (adverse selection): create signaling mechanisms (warranties, education credentials, guarantees) that allow informed parties to credibly reveal information, or screening mechanisms that separate high-quality from low-quality. For both: the solution is either REDUCING the information asymmetry (transparency, monitoring) or DESIGNING AROUND it (incentive-compatible contracts).
- **When to use:** When contractual or institutional relationships are undermined by information asymmetry; when moral hazard needs to be reduced through incentive design; when adverse selection threatens market quality; when understanding why some markets produce poor outcomes despite willing participants.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the TWO specific information problems that cause market failure and relationship dysfunction: hidden action (behavior after agreement) and hidden information (knowledge before agreement). Each requires different interventions—monitoring for hidden action, signaling for hidden information.
- **Limits:** Complete information symmetry is impossible. Monitoring and signaling have costs. Solutions can create new problems. Some information asymmetries are socially desirable (privacy). The models assume rationality that may not hold.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in information economics (Akerlof's lemons problem, Arrow's hidden action), principal-agent theory (Jensen & Meckling), and contract theory (Hart & Holmström—Nobel 2016). Draws on moral hazard and adverse selection research, mechanism design, and incentive compatibility theory.

### Recombination · #441 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Scott Page's formal model of innovation as the combination of existing ideas, technologies, or approaches in novel ways—rather than creation from nothing. Most innovation is recombinant: the printing press combined the wine press and movable type; the iPhone combined a phone, music player, and internet browser; Uber combined GPS, smartphones, and the taxi model. The mathematical implication: the number of possible recombinations grows COMBINATORIALLY with the number of available components—meaning that expanding the component library (through cross-domain learning) produces exponentially more innovative possibilities. This is why cognitively diverse teams produce more innovation: they have more components to recombine.
- **How it helps:** Expand your library of 'components' (ideas, frameworks, techniques from different domains) to increase your recombinant innovation potential. The practices: (1) Read and learn across domains—every new domain adds components that can be recombined with everything you already know. (2) For team innovation: assemble cognitively diverse teams whose combined component libraries are larger than any individual's. (3) Create structured recombination processes: 'what if we combined approach A from domain X with approach B from domain Y?' (4) Study historical innovations to see the recombinant structure—most 'original' ideas are novel combinations of existing elements.
- **When to use:** When innovation is needed and 'thinking harder' isn't producing breakthroughs; when cognitive diversity would expand the library of recombinant components; when cross-domain learning would create novel combination possibilities; when understanding that innovation is COMBINATORIAL rather than creative would change the approach to generating new ideas.
- **Unique value:** Provides the mathematical framework for why cross-domain learning produces innovation: each new domain's ideas MULTIPLY (not add to) the possible combinations with every other domain's ideas. This makes breadth of knowledge a mathematical asset for innovation, not just a cultural preference.
- **Limits:** Not all recombinations are valuable—most are useless. Identifying WHICH combinations have value requires domain expertise. Deep expertise is also necessary for innovation. The model can undervalue genuine originality. Recombination still requires insight to see which combinations work.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in evolutionary biology (recombination in genetics), combinatorial innovation theory (Weitzman's combinatorics of ideas), and complexity science. Draws on Kauffman's NK fitness landscapes, Schumpeter's recombinant innovation, and knowledge spillover theory. Applies the biological metaphor of crossover to idea generation.

### Replicator Dynamics · #442 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The mathematical model of how strategy frequencies change based on relative fitness—successful strategies become more common, unsuccessful ones rarer. Strategies above population average INCREASE in frequency; below average DECREASE. No central authority decides—dynamics emerge from differential success. In markets: profitable models attract imitators; unprofitable ones go extinct. In culture: successful ideas replicate more than unsuccessful ones.
- **How it helps:** Understand which strategies are replicating and why—this predicts your competitive landscape's future. What competitor strategies are being imitated? That's where the market converges. What ideas spread fastest? That's the future cultural landscape. Either BE the replicating strategy (for growth) or find the counter-strategy exploiting convergence (contrarian advantage).
- **When to use:** When understanding competitive landscape evolution; when predicting which strategies will dominate; when finding contrarian opportunities in converging landscapes; when modeling cultural evolution.
- **Unique value:** Provides the framework for how landscapes EVOLVE: no one decides which strategies win—differential success automatically changes composition. Success is self-reinforcing and failure self-eliminating WITHOUT coordination.
- **Limits:** Assumes frequency-independent fitness. Real replication involves mutation. Doesn't account for environmental change. Path dependence can override fitness. Biological metaphor has limits in social systems.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in evolutionary game theory (Maynard Smith & Price 1973), population dynamics (Lotka-Volterra), and mathematical biology. The replicator equation formalizes selection dynamics: successful strategies spread proportionally to their fitness advantage. Draws on Fisher's theorem and Price equation in genetics.

### Tipping Point · #462 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Malcolm Gladwell's popularization of the concept that social phenomena can spread like epidemics—small changes in context produce dramatic, nonlinear shifts in outcome when conditions reach a critical threshold. The three rules of epidemics: (1) The Law of the Few: a small number of highly connected people (connectors, mavens, salesmen) drive disproportionate spread. (2) The Stickiness Factor: the message must be memorable and actionable enough to compel behavior change. (3) The Power of Context: small environmental changes can produce large behavioral shifts. The tipping point reveals that social change is not gradual and proportional—it's nonlinear and discontinuous.
- **How it helps:** When trying to create social change: identify and leverage the three epidemic rules rather than pushing for incremental, broad-based change. The practices: (1) Find and influence the FEW people who connect to many others (rather than trying to reach everyone). (2) Make the message STICKY (specific, memorable, actionable—not abstract and forgettable). (3) Design the CONTEXT to support the desired behavior (environmental nudges rather than persuasion). The meta-strategy: tipping points are created by concentrating effort on high-leverage factors (connectors, stickiness, context) rather than spreading effort broadly.
- **When to use:** When social or behavioral change needs to reach critical mass; when identifying high-leverage individuals or contextual factors would be more effective than broad campaigns; when understanding nonlinear dynamics of social spread; when the 'epidemic' model of social change applies to the situation.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that social change follows epidemic dynamics—not gradual accumulation but sudden tipping when the right combination of messengers, message, and context aligns. This makes PRECISION (right people, right message, right context) more important than SCALE (reaching everyone) for triggering social change.
- **Limits:** The tipping point model oversimplifies complex social dynamics. Not all social change follows epidemic patterns. Identifying tipping points in advance is extremely difficult. The model can produce overconfidence about engineering social change. Gladwell's specific examples have been critiqued on methodological grounds.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Gladwell's synthesis of Granovetter's threshold models (1978), Schelling's segregation dynamics, and complex systems science. Draws on epidemiological contagion models, social influence theory, and bifurcation mathematics. Popularized the idea that small changes in key parameters trigger discontinuous social phase transitions.

### Economies of Scale · #483 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** As production volume increases, the cost per unit decreases. Fixed costs (factories, software, management) are spread across more units, and specialization becomes possible. Economies of scale explain why larger companies can offer lower prices, why industries consolidate, and why startups face a chicken-and-egg problem. However, beyond a certain point, diseconomies of scale emerge: bureaucracy, coordination costs, and loss of agility.
- **How it helps:** Essential for competitive strategy and pricing decisions. Helps determine minimum viable scale, predict industry consolidation, and understand when being small is actually an advantage (when diseconomies dominate).
- **When to use:** When evaluating competitive dynamics. When pricing relative to competitors of different sizes. When deciding between growth and profitability. When large competitors seem to have insurmountable advantages.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific analytical lens through 'Economies of Scale' that reveals patterns and dynamics invisible from conventional perspectives.
- **Limits:** Economies of scale have limits—diseconomies eventually emerge. Digital products have different scale economics than physical ones. Scale advantages in one area don't necessarily transfer to others.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in classical microeconomics (Adam Smith's pin factory, Marshall's economies), industrial organization theory, and management science (Chandler's scale and scope). Connects to transaction cost economics (Coase, Williamson) and platform economics where network effects create winner-take-all dynamics.

### Overfitting · #496 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** In statistics and machine learning, building a model so tailored to historical data that it captures noise rather than signal, performing well on past data but poorly on new data. The concept applies broadly: a strategy perfectly optimized for last year's conditions may fail in changed conditions. Overfitting happens when you have too many parameters relative to data points, or when you keep adjusting your approach until it matches history perfectly. The antidote is simplicity, out-of-sample testing, and regularization.
- **How it helps:** Provides quantitative frameworks for analysis, prediction, and optimization. Turns intuitions into testable models and vague ideas into precise tools.
- **When to use:** When analyzing data; when building predictive models; when evaluating statistical claims; when quantifying uncertainty.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific analytical lens through 'Overfitting' that reveals patterns and dynamics invisible from conventional perspectives.
- **Limits:** All models are simplifications that can mislead when applied beyond their valid domain. Context matters. Multiple models should be used together for robust analysis.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in statistical learning theory (Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension), information theory (minimum description length principle), and Bayesian model selection. Connects to philosophy of science (Occam's razor, parsimony) and regularization methods in machine learning. Bias-variance tradeoff formalizes the overfitting problem.

### Regression to the Mean · #507 · `high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Extreme outcomes tend to be followed by more moderate ones. An extraordinary performance is likely to be followed by a more ordinary one, not because of any causal mechanism but because extreme results typically involve some luck that won't repeat. This statistical phenomenon explains why the best-performing fund one year rarely repeats, why hiring based on exceptional interviews often disappoints, and why interventions after crises often seem to 'work' when they didn't do anything.
- **How it helps:** Prevents misattributing random variation to skill or intervention. Saves enormous resources spent on false signals: the 'hot' salesperson who was actually lucky, the training program credited for improvement that was just regression. Calibrates expectations after extreme events.
- **When to use:** After any extreme result (good or bad). When evaluating whether an intervention 'worked.' When projecting future performance from recent exceptional results.
- **Unique value:** The most commonly violated statistical principle in business decision-making. Understanding it prevents both false celebration and false despair.
- **Limits:** Not all extreme outcomes regress—genuine talent and systemic changes produce sustained extremes. The rate and extent of regression varies. Can lead to complacency if misapplied ('it'll just regress, no action needed').
- **Foundations:** Founded by Francis Galton (1886) through studies of heredity, grounded in bivariate normal distribution theory. Connects to correlation theory (Pearson), psychometrics, and econometrics. The phenomenon is a mathematical consequence of imperfect correlation, not a biological or psychological mechanism per se.
