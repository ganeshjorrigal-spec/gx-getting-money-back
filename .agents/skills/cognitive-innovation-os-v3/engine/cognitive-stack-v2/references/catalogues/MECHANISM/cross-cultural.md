### Karma as Causal Chain · #1857 · `high` · cross-cultural · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Karma is not fate but a causal theory of action. Every action produces consequences shaping future conditions, which shape future actions. The Gita distinguishes: the action itself (karma), intention (kartr), and accumulated results (karmaphala). Karma operates across timescales — immediate consequences, habit formation, and long-term dispositions (samskaras). Buddhism adds that wholesome karma (rooted in generosity, wisdom) differs from unwholesome karma (rooted in greed, hatred). The quality of intention matters more than outward form. Modern application: compound interest on behavior.
- **How it helps:** Karma is the original model of behavioral compound interest. Every decision creates conditions constraining or enabling future decisions. Each meeting, deliverable, and interaction compounds career capital. Entrepreneurs: every early decision about treating employees and customers creates samskaras (organizational habits) that compound. The intention dimension is crucial: actions from genuine goodwill create different dynamics than identical actions from self-interest, because intentions shape relationships and trust.
- **When to use:** When evaluating long-term compounding effects of seemingly small decisions. When culture or habits have accumulated and feel impossible to change. When considering not just what to do but with what intention. When you need motivation for consistent positive action despite individual instances seeming insignificant.
- **Unique value:** The most comprehensive ancient model of behavioral compound effects, integrating action, intention, habit formation, and long-term disposition. Unlike Western cause-and-effect thinking focused on immediate consequences, karma models multi-timescale accumulation.
- **Limits:** The cosmic dimension (karma across lifetimes) is unfalsifiable and can produce fatalism. Can justify social inequality — the poor 'earned' poverty. Overemphasis on individual karma ignores structural causes. The concept has been diluted in popular Western usage.
- **Foundations:** Founded in Buddhist philosophy, moral philosophy, and causal theory. Draws on dependent origination (pratityasamutpada), consequentialism, and moral psychology research. Connects to behavioral economics of long-term thinking, reputation effects theory, and agent-based modeling of social norms.

### Creolization (Cultural Mixing) · #1969 · `llm-high` · cross-cultural · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Originally describing how pidgin languages develop into full creole languages, creolization became a theory of cultural contact through Edouard Glissant. When cultures meet under conditions of inequality (colonialism, migration, globalization), they generate genuinely new forms irreducible to their components. Jazz is not 'African + European music' but a third thing born from their interaction under specific conditions. Key insight: creolization is unpredictable -- outcomes cannot be deduced from inputs. Unlike 'melting pot' metaphors implying homogenization, creolization preserves traces of originating cultures while producing irreducible novelty through actual contact, friction, and creative recombination.
- **How it helps:** Provides a more accurate model for cultural boundaries than assimilation (minority cultures dissolve) or parallel multiculturalism (cultures coexist separately). Helps businesses understand emergent cultures in cosmopolitan cities where market segments defy traditional categories. Explains innovation: breakthroughs often emerge from creolized spaces where unexpected combinations become possible. Helps policy-makers understand that immigrant integration produces neither cultural extinction nor preservation but something genuinely new that enriches the whole.
- **When to use:** When analyzing emergent cultural phenomena at contact zones -- immigrant neighborhoods, global cities, online spaces, post-colonial societies. When 'multicultural marketing' fails because communities do not fit neat categories. When understanding cultural innovation, hybrid identities, or new art forms that defy existing classification.
- **Unique value:** Captures the generative, unpredictable nature of cultural contact that static frameworks miss. Where Hofstede maps dimensions, creolization tracks dynamic creation processes. It centers power dynamics and historical conditions, showing that cultural mixing under colonialism differs fundamentally from mixing under equal exchange.
- **Limits:** Can romanticize cultural mixing while ignoring the violence driving it (slavery, forced displacement). The concept risks co-optation to market 'fusion' products that extract without reciprocity. Distinguishing creolization from cultural appropriation is politically contested. Not all cultural contact is generative.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Caribbean cultural theory (Glissant's creolite movement), sociolinguistics of creole languages, and postcolonial studies. Connects to Bakhtin's dialogism, hybridity theory (Bhabha), and cultural evolution research. Draws on jazz ethnomusicology, diaspora studies, and emergence theory in complex adaptive systems.
