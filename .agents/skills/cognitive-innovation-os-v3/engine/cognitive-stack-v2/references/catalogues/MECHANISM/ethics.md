### Ethical Fading · #1682 · `llm-high` · ethics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Ethical fading occurs when the moral dimensions of a decision gradually become invisible, typically through psychological mechanisms that reframe ethical choices as purely business, technical, or pragmatic ones. Ann Tenbrunsel's research shows that people don't typically make conscious decisions to be unethical—instead, the ethical dimension of their choice fades from awareness through euphemistic language ('creative accounting' instead of 'fraud'), incremental escalation (each small step is below the ethical threshold), outcome-focused reasoning ('the ends justify the means'), and role-based compartmentalization ('I'm just doing my job').
- **How it helps:** Build specific defenses against ethical fading into your decision-making process. First, use plain language: replace euphemisms with direct descriptions of what's actually happening. 'Restructuring the workforce' is 'firing 200 people'—naming it accurately keeps the ethical dimension visible. Second, evaluate incremental decisions against your starting position, not just the previous step: 'If someone had proposed this on day one, would I have agreed?' Third, periodically ask: 'If this decision were reported on the front page of the newspaper, would I be comfortable?' Fourth, designate a 'moral canary' in your team—someone whose role is to flag when ethical dimensions are being ignored.
- **When to use:** When you notice euphemistic language being used to describe potentially harmful actions. When decisions that would have been unthinkable a year ago now seem routine. When business pressure is high and 'practical' reasoning is overriding all other considerations.
- **Unique value:** Explains how ethical violations occur without conscious unethical intent—the most common and dangerous pathway to organizational misconduct. Uniquely identifies the specific psychological mechanisms (euphemism, incrementalism, role compartmentalization) that make ethics invisible.
- **Limits:** Diagnosing ethical fading in yourself is inherently difficult because the mechanism makes it invisible. Excessive vigilance can paralyze decision-making or create a culture of accusation. Some euphemisms are legitimate professional language, not ethical camouflage.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in behavioral ethics, social cognition, and organizational behavior research. Draws from Bandura's moral disengagement theory, Tavris & Aronson's self-justification research, Tenbrunsel and Messick's bounded ethicality, and Festinger's cognitive dissonance. Influenced by Milgram's obedience research and situational ethics.

### Moral Distress in Organizations · #1694 · `llm-high` · ethics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Moral distress occurs when you know the ethically right course of action but feel constrained from taking it by institutional pressures, hierarchical authority, or practical barriers. Unlike moral uncertainty (not knowing what's right), moral distress involves certainty about what's right combined with powerlessness to act on it. Originally identified in nursing (nurses who know the right treatment but can't override physician orders or hospital policy), moral distress applies broadly: engineers who know a product is unsafe but face pressure to ship, accountants who see irregularities but face retaliation for reporting, and middle managers forced to implement policies they believe are harmful.
- **How it helps:** If you're experiencing moral distress, name it specifically: 'I know what's right but I feel unable to do it because of [specific constraint].' This naming is the first step toward resolution. Then assess whether the constraint is real or perceived—often people overestimate the consequences of ethical action. If the constraint is real, seek allies who share your concern and build collective voice. Document your objections in writing (email) so there's a record. For leaders, moral distress in your organization is a leading indicator of ethical failure—if people feel constrained from doing what's right, it's a system design problem, not an individual weakness.
- **When to use:** When you feel certain about the right action but constrained from taking it. When team members are showing signs of cynicism, disengagement, or burnout that might stem from ethical rather than workload causes. When designing organizational systems and want to minimize the situations that force people into ethical compromises.
- **Unique value:** Identifies a specific condition—knowing what's right but being unable to act—that is distinct from moral uncertainty, burnout, or general stress. Uniquely reveals that organizational ethics failures often originate not in people's values but in systems that constrain their moral agency.
- **Limits:** Sometimes what feels like moral distress is actually moral uncertainty—you may not be as certain about the right course as you think. Not all institutional constraints are unjustified; some prevent impulsive ethical actions that would cause more harm. Individual perceptions of moral distress may differ from objective ethical analysis.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in nursing ethics (Jameton), organizational behavior, and moral psychology. Draws from moral philosophy's distinction between knowing and doing, role morality theory, institutional ethics research, and Rushton's moral resilience framework. Influenced by existentialist philosophy and workplace wellbeing research.
