### Bikeshedding (Law of Triviality) · #661 · `llm-high` · productivity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** C. Northcote Parkinson's observation that organizations give disproportionate weight to trivial issues while glossing over complex, important ones. Named after the example of a committee that spends hours debating a bike shed's color but minutes approving a nuclear reactor—because everyone can understand paint colors but few can engage with reactor design. The insight: the ease of forming an opinion is inversely proportional to the importance of the decision.

### Boiling Frog · #662 · `llm-high` · productivity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The metaphor that a frog placed in slowly heating water won't notice the danger until it's too late, while a frog dropped into boiling water would immediately jump out. While biologically inaccurate, the metaphor captures a genuine psychological phenomenon: humans are far better at detecting sudden changes than gradual ones. This explains how incrementally worsening situations—declining health, eroding relationships, organizational dysfunction—persist until crisis because no single step feels alarming.

### Cognitive Switching Penalty (Context Switching) · #664 · `llm-high` · productivity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The measurable productivity cost of shifting attention between tasks. Research shows each context switch requires 15-25 minutes to fully re-engage with the original task, and the error rate increases significantly during transitions. The implication: multitasking is an illusion—the brain is rapidly switching, not parallel processing—and the accumulated switching costs can consume 20-40% of productive time. Batching similar tasks and protecting focus blocks are the primary countermeasures.

### Desirable Difficulty · #668 · `llm-high` · productivity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Robert Bjork's counterintuitive finding that learning conditions that make initial performance worse often produce better long-term retention and transfer. Spacing practice over time (vs. massing), interleaving different topics (vs. blocking), and testing yourself (vs. rereading) all feel harder but produce superior learning. The insight: our subjective sense of learning fluency is a poor guide—easy-feeling study often creates an illusion of competence.

### Dual Coding Theory (Verbal + Visual) · #670 · `llm-high` · productivity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Allan Paivio's theory that information encoded in both verbal and visual formats is remembered significantly better than information in either format alone. The two systems are independent but interconnected—a concept described in words AND illustrated with a diagram creates two retrieval paths instead of one. This has direct implications for learning design: always pair explanations with visuals, diagrams with labels, and abstract concepts with concrete images.

### Spacing Effect · #693 · `llm-high` · productivity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The robust cognitive finding that distributing learning over time (spaced practice) produces dramatically better long-term retention than concentrating it in a single session (massed practice/cramming). Studying a topic for 30 minutes across 6 days produces far more lasting knowledge than studying for 3 hours in one day—despite identical total time. The spacing effect works because each retrieval after a gap strengthens the memory trace and forces deeper processing than re-reading in the same session. It's one of the most replicated findings in cognitive science.
- **How it helps:** Restructure all learning and skill development around spaced repetition. Use tools like Anki for factual knowledge, schedule skill practice in distributed sessions rather than marathon blocks, and revisit important concepts at increasing intervals (1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month). For organizations: replace day-long training events with distributed learning programs. For yourself: 20 minutes of daily practice beats a 3-hour weekend session for any skill. The spacing effect is the highest-leverage learning technique available—it multiplies retention per hour of study invested.
- **When to use:** When learning new material that needs to be retained long-term; when training programs aren't producing lasting behavior change; when skill development has plateaued despite significant practice time; when designing learning systems, onboarding programs, or educational content.
- **Unique value:** The single highest-ROI learning technique: simply distributing the same amount of study time over more sessions dramatically increases retention without increasing effort. Most learning systems are designed around massed practice (classes, workshops, bootcamps) because it's logistically convenient, not because it works—the spacing effect reveals this as a massive, systematic waste of learning time.
- **Limits:** Spaced practice requires more planning and discipline than massed practice. It's harder to schedule organizationally. For some types of learning (complex skills requiring sustained immersion), massed practice has advantages. The optimal spacing interval depends on the material and the desired retention period. Spaced repetition tools work best for factual knowledge; complex understanding may require different approaches.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve research (1885) and spaced repetition theory. Draws on distributed practice research, Bahrick's permastore concept, and Bjork's new theory of disuse. Related to desirable difficulties, long-term memory consolidation neuroscience, and testing effect amplification.
