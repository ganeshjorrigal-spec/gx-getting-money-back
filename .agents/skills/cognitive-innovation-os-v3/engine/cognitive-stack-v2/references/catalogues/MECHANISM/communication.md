### Medium Is the Message (McLuhan) · #126 · `llm-high` · communication · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Marshall McLuhan's insight that the medium through which content is delivered shapes the message more fundamentally than the content itself. Television didn't just deliver content to homes—it restructured family life, politics, and attention. The smartphone didn't just make calls portable—it restructured how humans relate to time, space, and each other. Every medium has inherent biases: text favors linear argument, video favors emotional narrative, Twitter favors provocation, TikTok favors performance. The medium you choose constrains and shapes what can be communicated through it.
- **How it helps:** Before crafting any communication, ask: what medium am I using, and how does that medium shape what can be said? A strategy presented via slide deck is constrained to bullet points and visuals; presented as a written memo, it can develop nuanced argument; presented in conversation, it can adapt to questions. The medium isn't neutral—it's a co-author. When your message isn't landing, the problem may be the medium, not the content. Long-form argument in a Slack message fails not because the argument is wrong but because Slack biases toward brevity and fragments attention.
- **When to use:** When choosing how to deliver important communications; when a message isn't landing despite good content; when evaluating how new media technologies are reshaping behavior and cognition; when designing communication strategies that need to account for medium effects.
- **Unique value:** Redirects communication strategy from content (what to say) to infrastructure (how the channel shapes what can be said). Most communication advice focuses on message crafting; McLuhan reveals that channel selection is the more fundamental strategic choice because it determines what kinds of messages are even possible.
- **Limits:** Content does matter—the medium shapes but doesn't completely determine the message. McLuhan's theory can be stretched to explain everything, which risks explaining nothing. New media blend characteristics of older media in unpredictable ways. The theory was developed for mass media and may apply differently to interpersonal communication.
- **Foundations:** Founded in media ecology and communication theory (McLuhan, Innis). Connects to technological determinism, semiotics, and sociology of knowledge. Influenced by gestalt psychology and extension of the nervous system metaphors in phenomenology.

### Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis (Language Shapes Thought) · #130 · `llm-high` · communication · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The hypothesis that the language you speak influences (or in the strong version, determines) how you think and perceive reality. Languages with different color vocabularies produce measurably different color perception. Languages with different spatial metaphors for time produce different temporal reasoning. In organizations: the vocabulary a company uses shapes what it can think about. If your organization has no word for a concept, the concept is harder to perceive, discuss, and act on. Mental models ARE a vocabulary—each model gives you a word for a pattern, making the pattern visible.
- **How it helps:** Expand your vocabulary to expand your perception. Every new mental model is a new word that makes a previously invisible pattern visible. 'Opportunity cost' isn't just a concept—it's a perceptual tool that makes you SEE trade-offs you'd otherwise miss. In organizations: the terms you introduce shape what your team can think about. Introducing 'technical debt' into a non-technical team's vocabulary makes invisible costs visible. Introducing 'switching costs' makes invisible competitive dynamics visible. Language is not just communication—it's cognitive infrastructure.
- **When to use:** When important patterns seem invisible to your team (they may lack the vocabulary); when introducing new frameworks or mental models to organizations; when cross-cultural or cross-disciplinary communication fails (different vocabularies, different perceptions); when evaluating how organizational language may be constraining strategic thinking.
- **Unique value:** Provides the theoretical foundation for why mental models matter at the cognitive level. Learning a mental model isn't just adding a tool—it's adding a perceptual category that makes previously invisible patterns visible. This elevates the mental models project from 'useful framework collection' to 'cognitive expansion.'
- **Limits:** The strong version (language determines thought) is largely rejected; the weak version (language influences thought) is supported but debated. Thought clearly exists without language (pre-verbal infants, mathematical intuition). New concepts can be expressed in any language through circumlocution. The relationship between language and thought is bidirectional and complex.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in linguistic anthropology (Sapir, Whorf) and philosophy of language. Connects to cognitive linguistics (Lakoff, Johnson), embodied cognition, and Vygotsky's sociocultural theory linking language to higher-order thought.
- *Secondary roles:* PROPERTY · *Collection:* —

### Double Bind Communication (Bateson) · #2227 · `llm-high` · communication · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Gregory Bateson's concept from schizophrenia research: a communication pattern where someone receives two contradictory messages simultaneously, cannot comment on the contradiction, and cannot escape the situation. The classic form: a parent tells a child 'come give me a hug' with stiff, rejecting body language—approaching is punished (by the body language) and not approaching is punished (by the verbal instruction). The recipient cannot win, cannot point out the contradiction, and cannot leave. In organizational contexts: 'Take risks and innovate! (But don't fail.)' 'Be honest with me! (But don't say anything I don't want to hear.)' 'Work-life balance is important! (But everyone who gets promoted works weekends.)' The double bind produces anxiety, confusion, and eventual learned helplessness because every response is wrong. The pattern is especially destructive when there's a power differential—the person in the lower-power position can't call out the contradiction without risk.
- **How it helps:** Provides a framework for identifying and naming impossible communication situations, which is itself the primary intervention—double binds survive by being invisible. (1) When you feel confused, anxious, and unable to respond correctly no matter what, check for contradictory messages. (2) Name the bind explicitly: 'I'm hearing X but seeing Y—which should I respond to?' This breaks the meta-communicative taboo that maintains the bind. (3) When you're the one sending double binds, check for alignment between your verbal and non-verbal messages, and between your stated values and your actual rewards. (4) In organizations, audit for institutional double binds by comparing official messaging with actual incentive structures.
- **When to use:** When you feel paralyzed by contradictory expectations. When anxiety in communication seems disproportionate to the content. When organizational messaging contradicts organizational behavior. When someone seems confused by what should be simple communication.
- **Unique value:** Bateson's framework explains a specific kind of communication toxicity that feels crazy-making but is hard to articulate without the model. The organizational application (stated values vs. actual incentives) makes it relevant to every workplace.
- **Limits:** True double binds require all three conditions (contradictory messages, can't comment, can't leave). Many uncomfortable communications are not technically double binds. Overuse of the concept can pathologize normal communication ambiguity. The original clinical context was controversial.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Bateson's double bind theory (1956, schizophrenia research), communication theory (Watzlawick's Pragmatics of Human Communication), and family systems therapy. Draws on metacommunication research, paradoxical injunction analysis, and Gregory Bateson's cybernetic framework.
- *Secondary roles:* STRUCTURE · *Collection:* anti-pattern

### DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender) · #2229 · `llm-high` · communication · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Jennifer Freyd's framework for a specific manipulation pattern used when someone is confronted with their harmful behavior: (1) Deny—'That didn't happen' or 'That's not what I did,' (2) Attack—the person who raised the concern is attacked ('You're being paranoid,' 'You're trying to destroy me'), (3) Reverse Victim and Offender—the original perpetrator positions themselves as the victim and the person who raised the concern as the aggressor ('Can't you see how much your accusation is hurting me?'). DARVO is deployed automatically by many people who harm others, from interpersonal abuse to institutional misconduct. Harvey Weinstein's response to allegations followed the DARVO script precisely. Institutional DARVO is common: organization harms someone → person reports it → organization attacks the reporter's credibility → organization positions itself as the victim of unfair accusations. The pattern's power: it exploits the natural human tendency to empathize with whoever is expressing pain, redirecting empathy from the actual victim to the perpetrator.
- **How it helps:** Provides a specific, named pattern that helps targets recognize they're being manipulated in real-time. The recognition itself is the primary defense—once you see the DARVO script playing out, the manipulation loses much of its power. Response strategies: (1) when you see Deny, document everything (written records resist denial), (2) when you see Attack, hold your ground—the attack is a signal you've hit something real, (3) when you see Reverse Victim/Offender, ask 'Who was harmed first?'—the timeline reveals the actual victim. For institutions: (1) train investigators to recognize institutional DARVO, (2) protect reporters from retaliation as a structural matter, not just a policy.
- **When to use:** When raising legitimate concerns and finding yourself suddenly positioned as the bad guy. When someone's response to being called out follows the deny-attack-reverse sequence. When institutions respond to reports of harm by attacking the reporter. During any situation where accountability is being evaded through manipulation.
- **Unique value:** The three-step sequence (Deny, Attack, Reverse) is specific enough to recognize in real-time, which is rare for manipulation frameworks. Freyd's research provides empirical backing. The institutional application extends the concept beyond interpersonal abuse to organizational dynamics.
- **Limits:** Not every denial is DARVO—sometimes people are genuinely innocent and defending themselves. False accusations do exist. The model can be weaponized to preemptively frame any denial as proof of guilt (itself a form of manipulation). Requires careful judgment.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in clinical psychology of narcissistic abuse (Freyd's DARVO concept, 1997), victim-offender dynamics, and forensic psychology. Draws on cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger), secondary victimization research, and institutional betrayal research (Freyd & Smith).
- *Secondary roles:* TRAJECTORY · *Collection:* anti-pattern

### Gossip Triangle (Information Laundering) · #2238 · `llm-high` · communication · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The communication pattern where information is routed through third parties to achieve objectives that direct communication cannot: influence without accountability, reputation damage without confrontation, or information gathering without revealing interest. The triangle: Person A tells Person B something about Person C, with the implicit or explicit understanding that B will convey it to C—but because A didn't tell C directly, A maintains deniability. Variations: using gossip to float ideas ('I heard someone say...'), to damage reputations without being the source ('Don't tell them I said this, but...'), to create social pressure without direct confrontation ('Everyone is saying...'), and to test reactions ('What would you think if someone did X?'). In organizations, gossip triangles create a shadow communication network that operates parallel to and often in conflict with official channels, making organizational diagnosis nearly impossible because the real information flow is invisible.
- **How it helps:** Provides specific interventions for both participants and targets of gossip triangles. As a potential carrier: (1) refuse to carry messages—'That sounds like something you should tell them directly,' (2) refuse to serve as audience for reputation attacks—'I'm not comfortable discussing someone who isn't here,' (3) when someone says 'Don't tell them I said this,' recognize that you're being recruited into a triangle. As a target: (1) when you hear 'everyone is saying,' ask 'Who specifically?'—this usually collapses the vague accusation, (2) go directly to the source rather than responding through the triangle. For organizations: (1) create norms that complaints and feedback travel directly, not through intermediaries, (2) leaders should never act on information received through gossip without verification from the source.
- **When to use:** When you're being used as an intermediary in others' conflicts. When you hear 'Don't tell them, but...' When organizational decisions seem to be made based on hallway conversation. When reputation damage is occurring through indirect channels.
- **Unique value:** Goes beyond 'gossip is bad' to map the specific triangle structure and the strategic functions it serves (deniability, reputation management, influence without accountability). The specific refusal scripts provide immediately usable tools.
- **Limits:** Not all indirect communication is gossip triangles—seeking advice about someone's behavior is legitimate. Some information genuinely needs to be shared through third parties for safety. Complete elimination of informal communication would be unhealthy. Context matters enormously.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in social network theory (Burt's structural holes), gossip research (Dunbar's social grooming hypothesis), and information laundering concept. Draws on indirect communication research, coalition formation theory, and power dynamics in organizational politics literature.
- *Secondary roles:* STRUCTURE · *Collection:* anti-pattern
