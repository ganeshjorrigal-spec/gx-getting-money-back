### Alloying · #640 · `llm-high` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Combining two or more metals creates an alloy with properties that none of the components possess individually. Bronze (copper + tin) is harder than either component. Steel (iron + carbon) is stronger and more flexible than pure iron. In life and business, alloying describes how combining distinct elements—skills, perspectives, team compositions, strategies—can create emergent properties superior to any individual component.
- **How it helps:** Explains why diverse teams outperform homogeneous ones, why multidisciplinary approaches yield breakthroughs, and why combining 'good enough' elements can produce something exceptional. Guides hiring, partnerships, and skill development toward complementary combinations.
- **When to use:** When building teams (seek complementary strengths, not clones). When developing strategy (combine approaches). When your individual capabilities feel insufficient—find what to alloy with.
- **Unique value:** Makes the case for diversity and combination at a material-science level. The emergent properties of alloys don't exist in any component—they only emerge from combination.
- **Limits:** Not all combinations produce useful alloys—some elements don't mix well. The proportions matter enormously (too much carbon makes steel brittle). Requires experimentation to find the right combination.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in materials science and metallurgy's solid-solution strengthening theory. Draws on complexity theory's emergence, systems theory's hybrid vigor concept, and evolutionary biology's heterosis. Related to innovation through recombination, boundary-spanning in organizational theory, and hybrid strength in materials engineering.

### Chain Reaction · #642 · `high` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A process where the products of one reaction trigger additional reactions in a self-sustaining or accelerating cascade—each event causes one or more subsequent events, producing exponential growth until a limiting factor intervenes. Nuclear fission is the paradigmatic chain reaction: one neutron splits one atom, releasing neutrons that split more atoms. In business: one customer referral leads to multiple new customers who each refer more (viral growth). One process failure triggers other process failures (cascading failure). One cultural norm violation normalizes further violations (broken windows cascade). Chain reactions are the mechanism behind both explosive growth and explosive collapse.
- **How it helps:** Identify where chain reaction dynamics exist in your system—both positive (viral growth, knowledge cascading, cultural momentum) and negative (cascading failures, trust erosion, quality degradation). For positive chains: invest in the initiation and remove friction from the propagation. Viral products need both a trigger (first use experience) and a transmission mechanism (easy sharing). For negative chains: install circuit breakers that stop cascading failures. In systems design: fail-safes, firewalls, and isolation boundaries prevent one failure from triggering a chain. The critical question: what's the reproduction rate? If each event triggers more than one subsequent event (R>1), you have exponential dynamics.
- **When to use:** When growth or decline seems to accelerate rather than stay linear; when one failure is triggering additional failures; when designing systems that need protection against cascading failure; when building products or movements that need viral dynamics.
- **Unique value:** Provides the physical mechanism behind all exponential dynamics: self-reinforcing cascades where each event produces multiple subsequent events. The reproduction rate (R) determines whether a chain sustains (R=1), accelerates (R>1), or fizzles (R<1)—making it the single most important parameter to understand and influence.
- **Limits:** Chain reactions always encounter limiting factors—no exponential process continues indefinitely. Identifying reproduction rates in social systems is much harder than in physics. Not all rapid growth or decline is chain-reaction-driven. The model can create false urgency about small perturbations that won't actually cascade.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in nuclear physics (fission chain reactions) and nonlinear dynamics. Draws on epidemiology's R-number models, complexity theory's autocatalytic sets (Kauffman), and Schelling's tipping point sociology. Related to viral dynamics, exponential growth theory, positive feedback loops, and cascade failure analysis.
