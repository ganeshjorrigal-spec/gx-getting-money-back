### Spamming · #960 · `llm-high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Flooding a communication channel with low-quality, unsolicited content to exploit attention at near-zero marginal cost to the sender but significant cost to receivers. Spam economics: if sending a million messages costs $100 and 0.01% convert, the spammer profits while 999,990 people bear the annoyance cost. As a general model, spamming describes any strategy where the sender externalizes costs to receivers—volume-based outreach, mass litigation, flooding regulatory processes with comments, or meeting culture where one person's 'just a quick meeting' consumes hours of others' time.
- **How it helps:** Audit your own communication for spam characteristics: are you sending messages that are costless for you but costly for recipients? Mass emails, excessive Slack messages, unnecessary meetings, and 'FYI' forwards are all organizational spam. The test: would you send this if it cost you $10 per recipient? If not, you're probably externalizing costs. For defense: build filters (both technical and social) that raise the cost of low-value communication and protect attention as the scarce resource it is.
- **When to use:** When communication channels are overwhelmed with low-value content; when meeting calendars are full but productivity is low; when designing outreach strategies and need to avoid spam economics; when organizational communication practices externalize attention costs.
- **Unique value:** Generalizes spam from email nuisance to universal economic model: any system where senders bear low costs while receivers bear high costs will be overwhelmed with low-quality input. This explains meeting culture, regulatory comment flooding, and mass outreach with one framework.
- **Limits:** The line between valuable outreach and spam is subjective—one person's spam is another's useful information. Volume-based strategies sometimes work and aren't always exploitative. Anti-spam measures can block legitimate communication. Some channels are designed for broadcast (social media) where the spam model applies differently.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in information economics, attention economics (Herbert Simon), and externality theory in welfare economics (Pigou). Draws on public goods theory, communication channel theory, and behavioral economics on cognitive load. Connects to network economics and Tragedy of the Commons (Hardin).

### Tocqueville Effect (Rising Expectations) · #961 · `llm-high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Alexis de Tocqueville's observation that revolutions happen not when conditions are worst but when conditions are improving—because improvement raises expectations faster than reality can satisfy them. People compare their situation not to the past (which was worse) but to the newly visible possibility of something better. In organizations: morale crises often follow periods of improvement, because employees who have experienced progress now expect more. Satisfaction is determined by the gap between expectation and reality, not by absolute conditions.
- **How it helps:** When managing change or improvement, anticipate the Tocqueville Effect: success creates rising expectations that can outpace continued improvement, producing dissatisfaction despite objectively better conditions. The intervention: manage expectations alongside improvements. Communicate progress AND remaining challenges. When conditions improve, people don't feel grateful—they feel entitled to more improvement. This isn't ingratitude; it's the predictable psychology of rising expectations. Plan for it rather than being surprised by it.
- **When to use:** When employee satisfaction drops despite objective improvements; when successful change initiatives generate more complaints rather than fewer; when managing transitions and need to understand why 'things are better but people are unhappier'; when planning the communication strategy around organizational improvements.
- **Unique value:** Explains the counterintuitive paradox that improvement can produce dissatisfaction. Most managers expect gratitude after improvements; the Tocqueville Effect predicts the opposite—rising expectations outstrip rising conditions. Understanding this dynamic prevents leaders from being blindsided by increasing complaints during improving conditions.
- **Limits:** Not every post-improvement dissatisfaction is the Tocqueville Effect—sometimes new complaints are about genuinely new problems. The effect is more descriptive than predictive—it doesn't specify how fast expectations will rise. In truly dire conditions, improvement usually does increase satisfaction. The model can be used to dismiss legitimate grievances as 'just rising expectations.'
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Alexis de Tocqueville's political sociology ('Democracy in America', 1835) and relative deprivation theory (Stouffer, Gurr). Draws on social comparison theory (Festinger), aspiration-achievement gap research, and behavioral economics on reference dependence. Connects to Davies' J-Curve of revolution.

### Mimetic Theory (René Girard) · #972 · `llm-high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Girard's theory that human desire is fundamentally imitative—we don't want things intrinsically but because we see others wanting them. We desire according to the desire of the Other. This explains why competition intensifies between similar rivals (mimetic rivalry), why social media creates desire spirals (everyone wanting what influencers have), and why organizational conflicts between similar departments are often more intense than conflicts between different ones. Mimetic desire also explains bubbles, fashion cycles, and the violence that emerges when rivals become indistinguishable.
- **How it helps:** Audit your desires: how many of your goals are genuinely self-generated versus inherited from mimetic models (peers, competitors, social media figures)? In business: mimetic theory explains competitive herding (everyone pursuing the same market because everyone else is), hiring FOMO (wanting candidates because competitors want them), and strategic imitation (copying competitors instead of differentiating). Peter Thiel uses Girard's framework to argue for contrarian strategy: pursue what nobody else wants, and you avoid mimetic competition entirely.
- **When to use:** When desires feel urgent but lack clear personal origin; when competition is intensifying between similar rivals; when herd behavior is driving market or organizational decisions; when evaluating whether a strategic direction is genuinely valuable or just mimetically attractive because competitors are pursuing it.
- **Unique value:** Goes deeper than behavioral economics by questioning the origin of desire itself. Most frameworks take 'what you want' as given and optimize how to get it. Girard questions whether what you want is genuinely yours—a more radical and often more useful intervention that can redirect entire strategy trajectories.
- **Limits:** Not all desire is mimetic—biological needs, genuine curiosity, and intrinsic calling exist. Girard's theory can become reductive if applied to ALL motivation. The scapegoat mechanism (the resolution of mimetic crisis) is more controversial and less empirically supported than the basic mimetic desire thesis. Telling people their desires aren't real can feel dismissive.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in René Girard's mimetic theory ('Violence and the Sacred', 1972; 'Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World', 1978). Draws on social psychology of imitation (Tarde), scapegoat anthropology, and literary theory. Connects to behavioral economics on herding, reflexivity theory (Soros), and evolutionary psychology.

### Role Theory (Social Roles Shape Behavior) · #974 · `llm-high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The sociological insight that behavior is primarily shaped by the roles people occupy rather than by their individual personality traits. A person behaves differently as a parent, employee, friend, and customer—not because they're being inconsistent but because each role carries distinct expectations, norms, and scripts. Role theory explains why the same person can be a tyrant at work and gentle at home, or innovative in one context and conformist in another. The role, not the person, is the primary unit of behavioral analysis.
- **How it helps:** When trying to change behavior—your own or others'—change the role rather than trying to change the person. Giving someone the title and explicit responsibilities of 'innovation lead' changes their behavior more reliably than asking them to 'be more innovative.' Role-based interventions work because they change the social expectations, accountability structures, and identity scripts that drive behavior. In organizational design: define roles clearly, and you define behavior more effectively than any amount of training.
- **When to use:** When someone's behavior seems inconsistent across contexts (they're occupying different roles); when individual behavior change efforts fail (the role may be overriding personal intention); when designing organizational structures and need to predict behavioral outcomes; when conflict arises from unclear or conflicting role expectations.
- **Unique value:** Shifts the behavioral change target from personality to structure. Instead of asking 'how do I change this person?' ask 'what role are they in, and what behavior does that role produce?' This is both more explanatory (why behavior varies across contexts) and more actionable (redesign the role to redesign the behavior).
- **Limits:** People aren't purely role-determined—personality, values, and agency influence how roles are enacted. Reducing people to their roles can feel dehumanizing. Role theory works better for explaining typical behavior than exceptional behavior. Some people actively resist role expectations, and that resistance can be valuable.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in structural sociology and symbolic interactionism. Draws on Erving Goffman's dramaturgical theory ('The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life', 1959), Talcott Parsons's structural-functionalism, and Ralph Turner's role theory. Connects to identity theory, organizational behavior research, and social psychology of role conflict.

### Political Opportunity Structure · #1744 · `llm-high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Political opportunity structure (POS) theory explains why movements emerge when they do—not when grievances intensify but when political openings appear. Sidney Tarrow identifies key dimensions: elite divisions (splits creating potential allies), institutional access (new participation channels), declining repression (reduced protest costs), and influential allies. The theory explains why movements emerge during improvement rather than worst conditions—'rising expectations.' It explains why identical grievances produce movements in some countries but not others, and why protest waves cluster temporally. POS adds a crucial variable: available political opportunities determine whether grievances become movements.
- **How it helps:** Don't just ask 'what should change?' but 'what political opportunities exist now?' Look for elite divisions (politicians breaking ranks on your issue), institutional access points (elections, regulatory comment periods), and potential allies. For entrepreneurs in advocacy tech, POS explains market timing: products serving emerging movements succeed when political opportunities align. Understanding that change depends on windows of opportunity explains why persistent advocacy sometimes suddenly succeeds.
- **When to use:** When deciding where to invest civic effort for maximum impact. When analyzing why some movements succeed and others fail despite similar grievances. When timing advocacy campaigns. When evaluating whether current conditions favor specific types of change.
- **Unique value:** Explains why the same level of grievance produces collective action at some times and not others, providing a structural analysis of when windows for change open—making civic strategy timing-aware rather than grievance-focused alone.
- **Limits:** POS can become tautological ('movements succeed when conditions are favorable'). Overemphasizes structural factors at the expense of agency, leadership, and framing. The specific dimensions of opportunity structure vary across contexts and are difficult to measure ex ante. Some movements create their own opportunities rather than waiting for them.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in social movement theory, political sociology, and comparative politics. Draws from McAdam's political process model, Tarrow's contentious politics framework, Tilly's mobilization theory, and Eisinger's original opportunity concept. Influenced by structuralist political science and resource mobilization theory.
