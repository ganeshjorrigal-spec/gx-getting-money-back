### Common Knowledge · #947 · `llm-high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A game theory concept describing information that everyone knows AND everyone knows that everyone knows (and so on, recursively). Common knowledge is more powerful than private knowledge because it enables coordinated action. Everyone may privately know the emperor has no clothes, but until someone says it publicly—making it common knowledge—no one acts on it. Announcements, public commitments, and visible signals create common knowledge that enables groups to coordinate in ways that private, distributed knowledge cannot.
- **How it helps:** When you need to change group behavior, make the relevant information common knowledge—not just known, but known to be known. This is why public announcements change behavior when private conversations don't. 'Everyone knows our culture is toxic' is private knowledge that produces no change; a CEO publicly naming the problem makes it common knowledge that demands response. In strategy: creating common knowledge (public commitments, announced deadlines, visible metrics) enables coordination that private information cannot.
- **When to use:** When an 'open secret' isn't changing behavior (it's not yet common knowledge); when you need to coordinate action across a group; when public commitments or announcements are more effective than private ones; when understanding why making private knowledge public can be transformative.
- **Unique value:** Explains the paradox of 'everyone knows but nobody acts.' The missing ingredient is almost always the shift from distributed private knowledge to common knowledge. This model reveals why transparency, public announcements, and visible metrics are not just communication tactics but coordination mechanisms with fundamentally different effects than private information sharing.
- **Limits:** Making information common knowledge can be destructive—some social fiction serves important purposes. Public exposure of problems can create panic rather than coordinated action. The recursive definition (knowing that everyone knows that everyone knows...) is theoretically precise but practically murky. Some coordination problems require more than common knowledge.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in game theory (common knowledge vs. mutual knowledge distinction, Aumann), epistemic logic, and philosophy of language (Grice's cooperative principle). Draws on Schelling's coordination theory, political economy research on public information effects, and social psychology's pluralistic ignorance research.

### Legibility (James C. Scott) · #953 · `high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Scott's insight that states and large organizations must make complex realities 'legible'—simplified and standardized enough to be measured, managed, and controlled from above. Last names, street grids, standardized measurements, and forest monocultures all make local complexity legible to central authority. The cost: legibility destroys the local, tacit knowledge embedded in complex systems. High-modernist projects fail when they impose legible simplicity on illegible complexity—planned cities that ignore how people actually live, corporate metrics that ignore how work actually gets done.
- **How it helps:** When designing measurement, management, or reporting systems, ask: what local knowledge is being destroyed to make this legible? Every dashboard, KPI, and standardized process simplifies reality for the benefit of the viewer at some cost to the viewed. Goodhart's Law (a measure becomes a target) is a legibility failure: the metric was supposed to represent reality, but people optimize for the metric instead. Build systems that preserve some illegibility—qualitative judgment, local autonomy, exceptions to rules.
- **When to use:** When standardizing processes and need to understand what's lost in standardization; when metrics seem to be capturing less and less of what actually matters; when central management decisions consistently misunderstand ground-level reality; when 'best practices' imposed from above fail in local contexts.
- **Unique value:** Reveals the hidden cost of management visibility. Every organization needs legibility to function at scale, but legibility always destroys local knowledge. The model doesn't argue against measurement—it argues for awareness of what measurement sacrifices and for preserving space for the illegible.
- **Limits:** Some legibility is genuinely necessary—complex organizations can't function without shared metrics and standards. The model can romanticize local knowledge that is sometimes simply inefficient. Not all 'illegible' practices contain hidden wisdom. Finding the right balance between legibility and local autonomy is a judgment call, not a formula.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in political anthropology and critical theory, drawing on James C. Scott's 'Seeing Like a State' (1998) and Michael Polanyi's concept of tacit knowledge. Connects to Hayek's knowledge problem in economics, Goodhart's Law in statistics, and ethnomethodology's study of practical reasoning in local contexts.
- **Recognition cue:** "Who benefits from this being more legible, and what gets lost?"
- **Worked example:** Standardized last names, scientific forestry, urban grid planning, KPIs replacing tacit knowledge

### Anomie (Normlessness) · #963 · `llm-high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Durkheim's concept describing the breakdown of social norms and values that leaves individuals without clear behavioral guidance, producing anxiety, alienation, and social dysfunction. Anomie occurs during rapid social change when old norms dissolve faster than new ones form—technological disruption, economic upheaval, cultural revolution. In organizations, anomie appears during poorly managed transitions: after acquisitions, leadership changes, or strategy pivots where old rules no longer apply but new ones haven't been established.
- **How it helps:** During any rapid transition—organizational restructuring, market disruption, cultural change—watch for anomie symptoms: rising anxiety, confusion about expectations, declining performance, and increased conflict. The treatment is establishing new norms quickly, even imperfect ones. In the absence of clear expectations, people don't innovate—they freeze or fragment. Leaders who move through transitions without explicitly re-establishing behavioral norms create anomie that looks like individual failure but is actually structural normlessness.
- **When to use:** During organizational transitions where old rules no longer apply; when team performance drops after leadership, strategy, or structural changes; when rapid social or technological change creates widespread confusion about 'how things work now'; when designing change management processes.
- **Unique value:** Names the specific dysfunction that occurs in the gap between old norms and new ones. Most change management focuses on the destination; anomie reveals that the transition itself—the period of normlessness—is where the real damage occurs and the real intervention is needed.
- **Limits:** Some degree of normlessness can be creative—too much stability prevents innovation. Anomie is a macro-level concept that may not explain individual experiences well. The solution (impose new norms quickly) can conflict with the need for participatory norm-building. Different people have different tolerance for ambiguity.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Émile Durkheim's foundational sociology ('Suicide', 1897; 'The Division of Labour', 1893). Draws on structural-functionalism, social integration theory, and Merton's strain theory. Connects to organizational behavior research on change management, identity disruption, and psychological safety (Edmondson).

### Habitus (Embodied Dispositions) · #968 · `llm-high` · sociopolitical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Bourdieu's concept that social conditioning becomes literally embodied—our posture, accent, tastes, mannerisms, and automatic responses reflect our social origins and training. Habitus operates below conscious awareness: a working-class person in an elite boardroom feels physically uncomfortable not because of explicit exclusion but because their embodied dispositions don't match the environment's implicit norms. Habitus explains why social mobility is harder than just acquiring credentials—you must also acquire the embodied cultural knowledge that insiders learn unconsciously.
- **How it helps:** Recognize that 'cultural fit' in organizations often means habitus match—not skill match. People who share the social background of current leaders feel comfortable and are perceived as competent; those from different backgrounds may be equally skilled but feel (and appear) out of place. Building genuinely inclusive organizations requires making implicit norms explicit so they can be learned, rather than treated as innate qualities that some people 'just have.'
- **When to use:** When 'cultural fit' assessments seem to consistently favor certain backgrounds; when talented people from different social origins struggle in your organization despite having the skills; when designing onboarding that helps diverse hires succeed; when examining your own automatic responses to people from different backgrounds.
- **Unique value:** Explains the mechanism of invisible social exclusion. Unlike explicit discrimination, habitus operates through comfort and discomfort—no one intends to exclude, but embodied norms create an environment where some people feel natural and others feel foreign. Making this mechanism visible is the first step to addressing it.
- **Limits:** Habitus can be deterministic—Bourdieu sometimes implies people can't escape their conditioning. In practice, people do change their habitus through exposure and practice. The concept can be over-applied to explain all social inequality as embodied conditioning. Individual agency and conscious adaptation are underweighted in the original theory.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Pierre Bourdieu's practice theory ('The Logic of Practice', 1980; 'Distinction', 1979). Draws on phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty's embodied cognition), social reproduction theory, and ethnography of social class. Connects to implicit bias research in psychology, organizational behavior, and diversity and inclusion frameworks.
- *Secondary roles:* MECHANISM · *Collection:* management-doctrine
