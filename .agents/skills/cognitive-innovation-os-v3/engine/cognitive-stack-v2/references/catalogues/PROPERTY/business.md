### Core Competency · #43 · `llm-high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Coined by C.K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel, a core competency is a deeply embedded organizational capability that provides access to multiple markets, contributes significantly to customer value, and is difficult for competitors to imitate. The strategic implication: companies should build strategy around their core competencies rather than around products or markets. The common mistake is confusing things you do frequently with things you do uniquely well—a true core competency passes the test of being valuable, rare, and hard to replicate.
- **How it helps:** Directly applicable to building, launching, and scaling products and businesses. Helps prioritize actions, identify market opportunities, and build sustainable competitive advantages.
- **When to use:** When building or iterating on products; when evaluating market opportunities; when deciding resource allocation; when scaling operations.
- **Unique value:** Directly actionable for product and business decisions. 'Core Competency' provides a specific framework that has been validated in real-world startup and enterprise contexts.
- **Limits:** Business models are context-dependent—what worked in one market may fail in another. Survivorship bias colors our understanding. Execution matters as much as strategy.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Prahalad and Hamel's resource-based view of the firm (1990). Draws on Penrose's theory of firm resources, dynamic capabilities theory (Teece), and strategic management. Informed by Barney's VRIN framework. Foundation for inside-out strategy formulations and make-vs.-buy decisions in corporate strategy.

### Freeroll · #55 · `llm-high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A situation where you can capture upside without meaningful downside risk—borrowed from poker where a player can win a pot but cannot lose. In business strategy, freerolls are rare but valuable: situations where you've already secured your downside and any additional effort creates pure optionality. The mental model trains you to look for asymmetric bets and to structure deals where your worst case is acceptable. Nassim Taleb's concept of convexity captures a similar insight.

### Perceived Value · #77 · `llm-high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The subjective assessment of a product's worth to a customer, which often differs dramatically from its objective cost or utility. Pricing strategy, branding, packaging, and context all shape perceived value. The key insight is that value is not an intrinsic property of the product—it exists in the customer's mind and can be influenced. A $5 aspirin in a hospital feels like a fair price; the same aspirin for $5 at a pharmacy feels outrageous. Context creates value.

### Product-Market Fit · #78 · `llm-high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Marc Andreessen's concept describing the moment when a product satisfies strong market demand—customers are buying as fast as you can produce, usage is growing organically, and the product essentially sells itself. The key insight is that product-market fit is binary and obvious—if you're not sure whether you have it, you don't. Before product-market fit, nothing else matters; after it, most things are solvable. The founder's only job pre-PMF is to reach PMF as fast as possible.

### Secrets · #84 · `llm-high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Peter Thiel's concept from 'Zero to One': valuable truths that few people agree with or know about. Great businesses are built on secrets—insights about the world that are true but not yet widely understood. Thiel distinguishes natural secrets (about the physical world) from human secrets (about what people want or do). The insight is that most people have stopped looking for secrets because they assume everything important has been discovered—but contrarian truths still exist and are the foundation of outsized value creation.

### Throughput · #96 · `llm-high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The rate at which a system produces its desired output, borrowed from manufacturing (Theory of Constraints) and applied broadly. The key insight is that throughput of the entire system is governed by the bottleneck—improving any non-bottleneck step produces zero improvement in overall throughput. This makes bottleneck identification the highest-leverage diagnostic activity. In knowledge work, the bottleneck is often decision-making or feedback loops, not task execution.
- **How it helps:** Directly applicable to building, launching, and scaling products and businesses. Helps prioritize actions, identify market opportunities, and build sustainable competitive advantages.

- **Foundations:** Rooted in Goldratt's Theory of Constraints and Throughput Accounting. Draws on operations research, queuing theory (Little's Law), and production economics.
