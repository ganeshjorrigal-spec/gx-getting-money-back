### Activation Energy · #639 · `llm-high` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The minimum energy required to start a chemical reaction. Before a reaction can proceed, an energy barrier must be overcome—even if the reaction would release energy overall. In life and business, activation energy explains why good things don't happen automatically: starting a new habit, launching a product, beginning a difficult conversation, or initiating organizational change all require an initial energy investment above the maintenance level.
- **How it helps:** Explains why knowing what to do and doing it are completely different things. Helps leaders design lower activation energy for desired behaviors (reduce friction to start) and higher activation energy for undesired ones. Critical for habit formation, product adoption, and change management.
- **When to use:** When people know what to do but aren't doing it. When designing onboarding, habit formation, or behavior change. When something beneficial isn't happening despite everyone agreeing it should.
- **Unique value:** Explains the gap between intention and action better than any other model. Most behavior change fails not because of bad goals but because activation energy wasn't addressed.
- **Limits:** Once a reaction starts, it may proceed in unexpected directions. Lowering activation energy for everything leads to impulsive action. Some high-activation-energy barriers serve protective functions.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in chemistry's transition state theory (Eyring, Arrhenius equation) and thermodynamics. Draws on enzyme kinetics research, energy landscape metaphors in complex systems, and biological activation thresholds. Related to tipping point theory, phase transitions in physics, and threshold effects in social systems.

### Critical Mass · #643 · `llm-high` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The minimum amount of something needed to trigger a self-sustaining chain reaction. In nuclear physics, it's the amount of fissile material needed for sustained fission. In business, it's the minimum number of users, revenue, or momentum needed for a product, community, or movement to become self-sustaining. Below critical mass, energy must be continuously added; above it, the system sustains and grows itself.
- **How it helps:** Provides the framework for launch strategy and community building. Helps determine the minimum viable audience, the tipping point for network effects, and the investment needed before a business becomes self-sustaining. Prevents premature scaling and explains why some products fail despite quality.
- **When to use:** When launching products, communities, or movements. When growth feels forced despite good fundamentals. When deciding how long to invest before expecting returns.
- **Unique value:** Grounded in fundamental physical laws that apply universally. 'Critical Mass' provides causal models that are more reliable than social-science generalizations.
- **Limits:** Critical mass is hard to determine in advance. Reaching critical mass doesn't guarantee sustained success—other factors must also be right. Different metrics may have different critical mass thresholds.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in nuclear physics (Manhattan Project critical mass concept) and nonlinear systems theory. Draws on Gladwell's tipping points, network theory's phase transitions, and epidemiology's herd immunity threshold. Related to threshold models in social science (Granovetter), diffusion of innovations (Rogers), and percolation theory.

### Leverage · #648 · `llm-high` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A small force applied at the right point can produce an outsized result. Archimedes: 'Give me a lever long enough and a fulcrum on which to place it, and I shall move the world.' In business and life, leverage means finding the points where effort multiplies: technology, systems, capital, relationships, skills, and timing. The greatest returns come not from working harder but from finding better leverage points.
- **How it helps:** The core operating principle for high-impact knowledge work. Helps identify where to invest time and resources for maximum return. Explains why some people and organizations achieve dramatically more with similar inputs—they've found better leverage.
- **When to use:** When allocating scarce resources. When choosing between multiple opportunities. When your effort feels disproportionate to your results (you may be pushing on a low-leverage point).
- **Unique value:** Transforms resource allocation from 'do more' to 'find the right fulcrum.' The single most important concept for maximizing impact per unit of effort.
- **Limits:** High leverage can amplify errors as easily as successes. Finding true leverage points requires deep understanding of the system. What provides leverage changes as conditions change.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in classical mechanics (Archimedes' lever principle) and mechanical advantage theory. Draws on Meadows's leverage points in systems thinking, capital structure theory in finance, and amplification in signal processing. Related to strategic leverage in business, force multiplication in military theory, and exponential returns.

### Velocity · #651 · `llm-high` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The rate of change of position with respect to time, including both SPEED (magnitude of motion) and DIRECTION. In physics: velocity is a VECTOR quantity (speed + direction), not just a scalar (speed alone). A car traveling 60 mph north has a different velocity than one traveling 60 mph east, even though their speeds are identical. As a mental model applied beyond physics: velocity thinking requires tracking not just HOW FAST you're moving but WHETHER YOU'RE HEADING IN THE RIGHT DIRECTION. Speed without direction is wasted motion. Many individuals and organizations optimize for speed (doing things faster) while neglecting direction (doing the right things)—producing high-speed movement toward the wrong destination.
- **How it helps:** Apply velocity thinking to any goal pursuit: assess both speed AND direction regularly. The diagnostic: 'Am I moving fast toward the right goal, or fast toward the wrong one?' For organizations: measure velocity, not just speed. A team that completes 100 tasks per week but none aligned with strategy has high speed and zero velocity. For personal development: 'busyness' is speed; 'productivity' is velocity. The practice: before optimizing for speed, CONFIRM DIRECTION. A 10% improvement in direction is worth more than a 50% improvement in speed when the direction is wrong.
- **When to use:** When speed is being confused with progress; when direction confirmation should precede speed optimization; when organizational busyness is substituting for strategic progress; when the vector nature of progress (magnitude + direction) needs explicit tracking.
- **Unique value:** Imports the physics distinction between speed (how fast) and velocity (how fast in which direction) to illuminate the most common productivity failure: optimizing for speed without confirming direction. This reframes 'moving fast' as potentially wasteful unless directionally correct.
- **Limits:** The physics analogy oversimplifies real-world progress. Direction isn't always clear in advance. Some exploration requires speed without clear direction. Over-emphasis on direction can produce analysis paralysis. Multiple valid directions may exist simultaneously.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in classical kinematics (speed with direction) and vector mathematics. Draws on strategic management's direction and speed framework, goal-setting theory (Locke, Latham), and behavioral economics' distinction between activity and purposeful movement. Related to execution discipline and strategic alignment theory.

### Center of Gravity (Where the Weight Concentrates) · #652 · `llm-high` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** From physics: the single point at which the entire weight of an object effectively concentrates—the point around which the object balances. In Clausewitz's military theory: the center of gravity is the source of an enemy's strength—the hub of all power and movement on which everything depends. Destroy it and the entire system collapses. Applied to business: every organization, market, or system has a center of gravity—the element that everything else depends on. For Apple it might be design excellence. For Amazon it might be logistics infrastructure. Identifying the center of gravity reveals both the greatest vulnerability and the greatest leverage point.
- **How it helps:** For any system you're analyzing (your own organization, a competitor, a market): identify the center of gravity—what does everything else depend on? In competitive strategy: attacking the center of gravity is more effective than attacking peripheral elements. In organizational development: strengthening the center of gravity produces disproportionate system-wide improvement. In product strategy: features that serve the center of gravity (core value proposition) matter more than peripheral features. The diagnostic: what ONE thing, if removed or degraded, would cause the entire system to fail?
- **When to use:** When strategic analysis needs to identify the highest-leverage intervention point; when competitive strategy needs to target the opponent's most critical dependency; when organizational investment needs to prioritize the element that everything else depends on; when simplifying complex systems by identifying the core around which everything else organizes.
- **Unique value:** Provides the specific military-strategic concept for identifying the single most important element in any system. Unlike general 'prioritization,' center of gravity analysis identifies the element whose failure cascades through EVERYTHING—making it both the highest-priority defensive asset and the highest-leverage offensive target.
- **Limits:** Complex systems often have multiple centers of gravity that shift over time. Identifying the true center requires deep understanding that surface analysis may miss. Attacking a competitor's center of gravity can trigger retaliatory escalation. Some systems are designed to be resilient to center-of-gravity attacks (distributed systems, redundancy).
- **Foundations:** Rooted in classical mechanics and statics (center of mass theory). Draws on military strategy (Clausewitz's Schwerpunkt), competitive strategy's core competency theory (Prahalad, Hamel), and structural engineering's load distribution. Related to resource concentration theory, strategic focus, and high-leverage point identification.
