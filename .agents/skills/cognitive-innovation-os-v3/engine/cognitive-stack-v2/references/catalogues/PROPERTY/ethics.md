### Ethics of Care (Gilligan & Noddings) · #1678 · `llm-high` · ethics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The ethics of care argues that moral reasoning is fundamentally relational, not abstract. Developed by Carol Gilligan as a critique of Lawrence Kohlberg's justice-oriented moral development theory, it centers the moral importance of relationships, empathy, and responsiveness to specific others' needs. Rather than applying universal principles to abstract cases, care ethics asks: who are the particular people involved, what relationships do they have, what are their specific vulnerabilities and needs, and what would maintain or strengthen those relationships? Nel Noddings distinguished natural caring (the motivation to care that arises from direct relationship) from ethical caring (the deliberate choice to maintain caring relationships when natural motivation falters).
- **How it helps:** Apply care ethics when universal principles seem to miss what matters in a situation. When deciding whether to fire a struggling employee, justice-based thinking might say 'performance metrics weren't met'; care ethics asks 'What's happening in this person's life, what does our relationship obligate me to do, and how can I respond to their specific needs?' This doesn't mean ignoring performance—it means considering the full human context. For leaders, care ethics illuminates the relational work that makes organizations functional: mentoring, emotional labor, conflict mediation, and culture maintenance.
- **When to use:** When a decision involves specific people you have relationships with and universal principles feel inadequate. When evaluating organizational practices that affect people's wellbeing and relationships. When justice-based reasoning produces a result that feels morally incomplete or tone-deaf to human relationships.
- **Unique value:** Centers relationships, context, and emotional attunement as moral considerations rather than treating them as distractions from abstract principles. Uniquely makes visible the relational and caregiving work that sustains organizations and communities.
- **Limits:** Can lead to favoritism—caring more for those close to you than for distant strangers. Provides little guidance for policy decisions affecting people you have no relationship with. Risk of burnout and self-sacrifice if caring is not reciprocal. Historically associated with women's moral reasoning, risking essentialism.
- **Foundations:** Emerged from feminist philosophy critiquing Kohlberg's justice-based moral development theory. Rooted in relational psychology, feminist theory (Chodorow), and maternal ethics. Draws from attachment theory (Bowlby), phenomenological ethics (Levinas's philosophy of the Other), and Nel Noddings's educational care ethics.
