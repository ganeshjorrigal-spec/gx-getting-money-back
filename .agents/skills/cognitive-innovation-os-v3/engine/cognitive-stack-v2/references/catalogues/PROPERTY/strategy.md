### Cheap Talk (Costless Communication) · #999 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** In game theory, communication that costs nothing to send and therefore can't be trusted as a credible signal—because lying has no penalty. 'I'll definitely refer business to you' costs nothing to say and therefore conveys no information about actual intent. In contrast, costly signals (investing time, money, or reputation) are credible because faking them has consequences. Cheap talk is ubiquitous in business: competitor threats, partnership promises, stated intentions, and customer commitments are all cheap talk until backed by costly action. The model explains why verbal agreements are weak and signed contracts are strong.
- **How it helps:** Evaluate all communication by its cost structure: is this person's statement backed by any costly commitment, or is it free to say? When someone says 'we should partner,' that's cheap talk. When they sign a letter of intent with a penalty clause, that's a costly signal. In your own communication: if you want to be believed, accompany words with costly actions. In negotiations: discount verbal commitments and focus on structural commitments (deposits, contracts, public announcements) that would be costly to renege on. The credibility of any statement is proportional to the cost of it being false.
- **When to use:** When evaluating the credibility of promises, threats, or stated intentions; when designing agreements that need to be enforceable; when distinguishing genuine commitment from performative enthusiasm; when analyzing whether competitive threats are credible.
- **Unique value:** Provides a precise framework for evaluating credibility: cost of lying determines credibility of the statement. This converts vague trust assessment into a specific, testable question—'what would it cost this person to be lying?'—that works in any communication context from business deals to personal relationships.
- **Limits:** Some cheap talk IS informative—people often tell the truth even without penalties for lying. Reputation serves as an implicit cost to lying, even for 'cheap' statements. Requiring costly signals for all communication would destroy normal human interaction. In repeated games, cheap talk becomes more informative because future interactions create implicit costs.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in game theory and information economics (Crawford and Sobel, 1982). Draws on signaling theory (Spence), mechanism design (Hurwicz, Maskin, Myerson), and strategic communication research. Connects to credibility theory in monetary economics, negotiation research, and political science on legislative communication.

### Evolutionary Stable Strategy (ESS) · #1002 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** John Maynard Smith's game theory concept: a strategy that, once adopted by a population, cannot be invaded by any alternative strategy—because any deviant strategy does worse against the established one. Hawks and doves: a population of all hawks fights too much (costly); a population of all doves is invadable by one hawk; the ESS is a specific RATIO of hawks and doves where neither can profitably increase. Applied to business: market equilibria where no firm can profitably deviate from the prevailing strategy. Cultural norms that are self-reinforcing because deviating from them is individually costly.
- **How it helps:** For any competitive environment: identify whether the current strategy mix is an ESS. If it is: deviation from the prevailing strategy will be punished (the market/culture will push you back). If it's not: the current mix is unstable and will shift toward an ESS. For strategy: you can either play the ESS (compete within the established rules) or try to shift the game to a new ESS where your strengths are better rewarded. The latter is much harder but produces lasting competitive advantage if successful. Disruption = establishing a new ESS.
- **When to use:** When competitive strategies have converged and deviation seems unprofitable; when understanding why markets reach stable configurations; when evaluating whether disruptive strategies can overcome the stability of current equilibria; when designing strategies that create new competitive equilibria.
- **Unique value:** Explains why competitive environments are stable: the ESS is self-reinforcing because any deviation is individually costly. This provides the formal framework for understanding why industries resist disruption (the current ESS punishes deviants) and what disruption actually requires (shifting to a new game with a different ESS).
- **Limits:** Real competitive environments are more complex than the models—multiple strategies can coexist. ESS analysis requires well-defined payoffs that real markets rarely have. The environment changes, shifting which strategies are evolutionarily stable. Finding a new ESS is much easier to describe than to accomplish.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in evolutionary biology (Maynard Smith and Price, 1973) and game theory. Draws on population dynamics, replicator dynamics in evolutionary game theory, and Nash equilibrium theory. Connects to behavioral economics on norms, cultural evolution research, and institutional economics on stable conventions.

### BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement) · #1034 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Roger Fisher and William Ury's concept from 'Getting to Yes': your best option if the current negotiation fails. BATNA is the single most important factor determining negotiation power—the party with the better alternative can walk away more credibly. The key insight: you should never accept a deal worse than your BATNA, and improving your BATNA (not just your negotiation skills) is the most effective way to improve negotiation outcomes.
- **How it helps:** Provides frameworks for navigating competitive landscapes, allocating resources under uncertainty, and making high-stakes decisions with incomplete information.
- **When to use:** When facing strategic decisions under uncertainty; when multiple stakeholders have competing interests; when the stakes are high and reversibility is low.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific strategic lens that reframes competitive dynamics. 'Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement' reveals options and constraints that are invisible from conventional perspectives.
- **Limits:** Strategic models assume rationality that real actors may not exhibit. Competitive situations are unique—historical patterns may not repeat. Over-analysis can paralyze action.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in negotiation theory and game theory (Fisher, Ury, and Patton's 'Getting to Yes', 1981; Nash bargaining solution). Draws on decision theory on outside options, game theory on credible threats, and behavioral economics on anchoring. Connects to labor economics, contract theory, and organizational behavior on negotiation.
- *Secondary roles:* RULE · *Collection:* negotiation-tactic

### Trade-offs · #1078 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Every choice involves giving something up to get something else. There is no free lunch—resources allocated to one use are unavailable for another. Trade-offs are not problems to be solved but realities to be navigated. The quality of your decisions depends on how clearly you see the trade-offs and how deliberately you choose which side to favor. Great strategy is explicitly choosing which trade-offs to accept.
- **How it helps:** Forces honest assessment of what you're giving up with every decision, not just what you're gaining. Prevents the dangerous illusion that you can 'have it all.' The foundation of strategic positioning—your strategy IS your trade-offs.
- **When to use:** Every significant decision involves trade-offs. Especially important when options seem cost-free, when 'and' thinking is being used to avoid 'or' decisions, and when resource allocation creates conflict.
- **Unique value:** The meta-model for all decision-making. Every other decision framework ultimately resolves into identifying and evaluating trade-offs.
- **Limits:** Can be used to justify inaction ('everything has trade-offs, so why bother'). Some apparent trade-offs can be dissolved through innovation. Trade-off analysis requires knowing the full set of consequences, which is rarely possible.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in neoclassical economics (opportunity cost, production possibility frontiers) and decision theory. Draws on philosophy of value pluralism (Isaiah Berlin). Informs engineering design theory and strategy (Porter's generic strategies). Related to Pareto efficiency and multi-objective optimization in operations research.

### Preserving Optionality · #1211 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The strategic principle of maintaining FUTURE CHOICES by avoiding premature commitment—keeping options open when uncertainty is high and the cost of reversing decisions is significant. Under uncertainty: the value of an option increases because it preserves the ability to choose later with more information. Irreversible decisions made under high uncertainty sacrifice the option value of waiting. The option-value framework: every decision that closes a door has an OPTION COST equal to the value of the information you'd gain by waiting. Nassim Taleb, financial options theory, and real options analysis all formalize this: optionality is valuable precisely BECAUSE the future is uncertain.
- **How it helps:** When facing uncertain decisions: assess the option value of waiting versus the cost of delay. High uncertainty + high irreversibility = preserve optionality (wait, experiment, keep doors open). Low uncertainty + easily reversible = commit and execute (optionality has low value). The practices: (1) Prefer reversible decisions over irreversible ones under uncertainty. (2) Invest in experiments that generate information before committing. (3) Design decisions as stages rather than single commitments. (4) Recognize that premature commitment under uncertainty is COSTLY, not decisive. For entrepreneurs: stage investments, validate before scaling, preserve the ability to pivot.
- **When to use:** When uncertainty is high and decisions are difficult to reverse; when the value of waiting for information exceeds the cost of delay; when commitment should be staged rather than absolute; when the option value of maintaining flexibility needs explicit valuation.
- **Unique value:** Provides the formal framework for valuing FLEXIBILITY: optionality has quantifiable value that increases with uncertainty and irreversibility. This transforms 'keeping options open' from vague advice into CALCULABLE strategy—and reveals when commitment IS the right choice (low uncertainty, easily reversible).
- **Limits:** Preserving optionality can become analysis paralysis. Some situations reward bold early commitment. The cost of delay may exceed the option value. Optionality has carrying costs. Excessive option preservation prevents the learning that comes from commitment.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in financial options theory (Black-Scholes, Merton), real options analysis (Dixit & Pindyck), and decision theory under uncertainty. Draws on flexibility valuation in engineering economics, portfolio theory (Markowitz), and the economics of information with irreversible decisions (Arrow).

### Self-Confidence · #1269 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The strategic and psychological resource of CALIBRATED belief in one's own ability to execute, learn, and adapt—distinct from both arrogance (overconfidence disconnected from evidence) and self-doubt (underconfidence disconnected from track record). True self-confidence is EVIDENCE-BASED: grounded in past performance, demonstrated learning ability, and honest assessment of both strengths and gaps. As a mental model: self-confidence is not a feeling but a CALIBRATION—the degree to which your belief in your abilities matches your actual abilities. Overconfidence leads to reckless commitment; underconfidence leads to missed opportunities. Calibrated confidence optimizes risk-taking.
- **How it helps:** Build calibrated self-confidence through systematic evidence collection. The practices: (1) Maintain a track record of predictions and outcomes to calibrate your confidence. (2) Distinguish between DOMAIN confidence (confident in areas with demonstrated competence) and GENERAL confidence (confident regardless of domain—dangerous). (3) Use past performance as the base rate for future confidence. (4) Separate confidence in your ABILITY TO LEARN from confidence in your CURRENT KNOWLEDGE—the former is more robust. For entrepreneurs: confidence should be highest where you have the deepest track record and most honest feedback loops.
- **When to use:** When confidence calibration would improve decision quality; when overconfidence or underconfidence is producing suboptimal risk-taking; when domain-specific confidence needs separation from general confidence; when evidence-based self-assessment would replace both arrogance and self-doubt.
- **Unique value:** Reframes self-confidence from a TRAIT (you have it or you don't) to a CALIBRATION (how well does your belief match your ability?). This makes confidence improvable through better evidence collection and calibration rather than through motivational pep talks.
- **Limits:** Perfect calibration is impossible. Some situations benefit from slight overconfidence. Confidence affects performance (self-fulfilling prophecy). Domain expertise doesn't always transfer. Calibration itself requires metacognitive skill.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in social psychology of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1977), positive psychology (Seligman), and clinical psychology on self-esteem (Rosenberg). Draws on attribution theory, performance psychology, and courage research (Rachman). Related to identity economics and the distinction between self-esteem and self-efficacy.

### Leverage (Negotiation) · #1317 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The relative power each party has in a negotiation, determined primarily by four factors: (1) alternatives (BATNA—your best option if this deal falls through), (2) information asymmetry (knowing something the other side doesn't), (3) time pressure differentials (who needs a deal sooner), and (4) relative cost of no deal (who is hurt more by walking away). Leverage is more important than negotiation tactics—a strong position with poor tactics beats poor position with strong tactics every time.
- **How it helps:** Before any negotiation, assess leverage on all four dimensions. Your BATNA is the foundation: the better your alternative, the more leverage you have—because you can genuinely walk away. Information is the second lever: understanding the other party's constraints, timeline, and alternatives gives you an advantage. The practical discipline: never enter a negotiation without knowing your BATNA and having taken steps to improve it. The best negotiation preparation is often improving your alternatives, not rehearsing tactics.
- **When to use:** Before any negotiation—business deals, salary discussions, vendor contracts, partnerships; when assessing whether to negotiate or accept terms; when a negotiation feels stuck—analyzing leverage often reveals why; when deciding whether to invest in improving your position vs. accepting the current terms.
- **Unique value:** Shifts focus from negotiation techniques to negotiation structure. Most negotiation advice focuses on what to say; leverage analysis focuses on what's true about your position. This is more actionable because you can change your leverage (by developing alternatives, gathering information, managing timelines) before the negotiation even starts.
- **Limits:** Leverage is dynamic—it changes as negotiations progress and new information emerges. Over-leveraging a position can damage long-term relationships. Leverage analysis assumes rational actors; emotional decisions can override leverage calculations. In ongoing relationships, wielding leverage aggressively creates resentment that extracts a later cost.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in negotiation theory (Fisher & Ury's principled negotiation, Raiffa's analytical framework) and Schelling's bargaining theory. Draws on power asymmetry analysis from political science and the BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement) concept from Harvard Negotiation Project.
