### Signaling Game (Costly Signals) · #1006 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A game theory model where one party has private information (their type, quality, or intention) and must communicate it credibly to another party who can't directly observe it. The solution: costly signals that only the genuine article can afford to send. A warranty signals product confidence because only a quality manufacturer can afford to honor it. An MBA signals ability because only capable students can complete the program. Advertising spending signals product quality because only profitable products can afford sustained campaigns. The informational content isn't in the signal itself—it's in the COST, which filters honest from dishonest signalers.
- **How it helps:** Design your signaling strategy around cost structures that only genuine quality can sustain. In hiring: what signals can genuinely skilled candidates produce that unskilled candidates can't fake? (Portfolio of real work > credentials > interviews > résumés, in descending order of signal cost.) In business: what actions demonstrate quality that mere claims can't? (Money-back guarantees, free trials, transparent pricing, public performance data.) When evaluating others' signals: calibrate trust to signal cost. Cheap signals (verbal promises, stated intentions) deserve less weight than costly signals (contractual commitments, resource investments).
- **When to use:** When information asymmetry exists and you need to communicate or evaluate hidden qualities; when designing hiring, partnership evaluation, or due diligence processes; when building credibility with skeptical audiences; when analyzing why some credentials or actions are more persuasive than others.
- **Unique value:** Provides the formal economic framework for 'actions speak louder than words.' The model specifies exactly WHY actions are more credible—their cost filters honest signalers from dishonest ones. This converts trust evaluation from gut feeling to structural analysis: how costly is this signal to fake?
- **Limits:** Signal costs can be discriminatory—expensive signals exclude capable but resource-poor signalers. Not all costly actions are informative signals. Signal inflation (credential creep) erodes the information content of once-reliable signals. The model assumes rational signal interpretation, but people are often poor at evaluating signal costs.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in signaling theory economics (Spence's job market signaling, 1973; Zahavi's handicap principle in biology). Draws on information economics, game theory of asymmetric information (Akerlof's lemon problem), and evolutionary biology. Connects to marketing theory, credibility research, and mechanism design.

### Stag Hunt (Cooperation vs Safety) · #1007 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A game theory model where two hunters must independently choose: cooperate to hunt a stag (high reward but requires both to participate) or individually hunt a hare (lower reward but guaranteed). The stag hunt captures a fundamental social dilemma: cooperation produces the best outcome but only if BOTH parties cooperate—and you can't be sure the other will. Unlike the Prisoner's Dilemma (where defection dominates), the stag hunt has two equilibria: mutual cooperation (stag) and mutual safety (hare). The challenge isn't incentive alignment—both prefer the stag—but trust and coordination.
- **How it helps:** Recognize stag hunts in your business and personal life: situations where cooperation produces the best outcome but requires trust that the other party will also cooperate. Joint ventures, strategic partnerships, team projects with shared accountability, and organizational change all have stag hunt dynamics. The intervention: build trust through small, verifiable cooperative acts before the big stag hunt. Signal your commitment credibly. Reduce the cost of cooperation failure (safety nets). Make the cooperative choice as safe as possible while preserving its benefits.
- **When to use:** When cooperation would produce the best outcome but trust is insufficient; when both parties want to cooperate but fear the other will defect to safety; when designing partnerships or team structures that require mutual commitment; when the barrier to collaboration is trust rather than incentives.
- **Unique value:** Distinguishes the trust problem (stag hunt) from the incentive problem (prisoner's dilemma). Many situations mistakenly analyzed as prisoner's dilemmas—where defection seems rational—are actually stag hunts, where both parties WANT to cooperate but can't trust that the other will. The intervention is completely different: build trust, don't change incentives.
- **Limits:** Real-world situations rarely map perfectly to the clean 2×2 stag hunt matrix. Trust is hard to build and easy to destroy. Some stag hunts have asymmetric risks that the simple model doesn't capture. The model assumes both parties equally prefer cooperation—in practice, preferences may differ.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in game theory and Rousseau's stag hunt parable in 'Discourse on Inequality' (1754). Draws on Brian Skyrms's analysis ('The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure', 2004), coordination game theory, and social contract theory. Connects to collective action theory, trust research, and evolutionary game theory.

### Prisoner's Dilemma (extended) · #1057 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The foundational game theory model where two rational agents, each acting in self-interest, produce an outcome that's worse for BOTH than if they had cooperated. Each prisoner can cooperate (stay silent) or defect (betray). If both cooperate, both get light sentences. If both defect, both get heavy sentences. If one cooperates and the other defects, the defector goes free and the cooperator gets the worst sentence. Individual rationality dictates defection—but mutual defection is worse than mutual cooperation. The dilemma captures the fundamental tension between individual incentives and collective welfare that pervades business, politics, and social life.
- **How it helps:** Recognize prisoner's dilemma dynamics in competitive and collaborative situations. The critical factors that shift outcomes toward cooperation: (1) Repeated interactions (Axelrod's tournaments showed tit-for-tat wins in repeated games). (2) Reputation (defection in one game spreads to other relationships). (3) Communication (discussing intentions before deciding). (4) Enforceable agreements (converting the dilemma into a coordination problem). In business: most competitive dynamics that feel like prisoner's dilemmas are actually REPEATED games where reputation matters—which dramatically changes the optimal strategy from defection toward conditional cooperation.
- **When to use:** When competitive dynamics are producing mutual harm; when two parties would both benefit from cooperation but neither trusts the other to cooperate; when designing agreements, partnerships, or market structures that need to overcome self-interested defection; when evaluating whether a competitive interaction is one-shot (favors defection) or repeated (favors cooperation).
- **Unique value:** The single most important model in strategic interaction: it reveals why individually rational behavior produces collectively irrational outcomes, and identifies the conditions (repetition, reputation, communication, enforcement) that shift equilibria toward cooperation. Understanding when you're in a prisoner's dilemma versus when you're not is the foundation of strategic thinking.
- **Limits:** Real-world interactions are rarely as clean as the 2×2 matrix. The model assumes rational actors with clear payoffs—reality is messier. Some 'prisoner's dilemmas' are actually coordination games or stag hunts with different optimal strategies. The model can justify preemptive defection ('they'll defect anyway'). Not all cooperation failures are prisoner's dilemmas.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in game theory (Tucker, 1950) and repeated game theory (Folk Theorem). Draws on Axelrod's cooperation research, mechanism design theory, and behavioral economics on reciprocity and punishment. Connects to institutional economics on contract enforcement, collective action theory, and evolutionary game theory on social norms.

### SWOT Analysis · #1064 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A strategic planning tool that maps Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats in a 2x2 matrix (internal/external × positive/negative). While ubiquitous, SWOT is most valuable as a conversation starter, not a decision tool—it identifies factors but doesn't weight them or suggest strategy. The common failure is listing factors without analyzing interactions: the most strategic insights come from matching strengths to opportunities and identifying weaknesses that expose you to threats.
- **How it helps:** Provides frameworks for navigating competitive landscapes, allocating resources under uncertainty, and making high-stakes decisions with incomplete information.
- **When to use:** When facing strategic decisions under uncertainty; when multiple stakeholders have competing interests; when the stakes are high and reversibility is low.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific strategic lens that reframes competitive dynamics. 'SWOT Analysis' reveals options and constraints that are invisible from conventional perspectives.
- **Limits:** Strategic models assume rationality that real actors may not exhibit. Competitive situations are unique—historical patterns may not repeat. Over-analysis can paralyze action.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in strategic planning and business policy, originating with the Harvard Business School (Andrews, 1971). Draws on industrial organization economics (Porter's five forces), resource-based view (Barney), and PEST analysis. Connects to strategic management theory, competitive analysis frameworks, and organizational capability assessment.
- **Recognition cue:** "Which quadrant is this factor?"
- **Worked example:** Strategic planning, competitive analysis, project review

### Satisficing vs. Maximizing · #1065 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Herbert Simon's distinction between choosing the first option that meets your criteria (satisficing) and exhaustively searching for the best possible option (maximizing). Research by Barry Schwartz shows that maximizers achieve marginally better objective outcomes but significantly worse subjective outcomes—they're less satisfied, more stressed, and more prone to regret. The paradox of choice: more options make maximizing harder and satisficing more rational.
- **How it helps:** Provides frameworks for navigating competitive landscapes, allocating resources under uncertainty, and making high-stakes decisions with incomplete information.
- **When to use:** When facing strategic decisions under uncertainty; when multiple stakeholders have competing interests; when the stakes are high and reversibility is low.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific strategic lens that reframes competitive dynamics. 'Satisficing vs Maximizing' reveals options and constraints that are invisible from conventional perspectives.
- **Limits:** Strategic models assume rationality that real actors may not exhibit. Competitive situations are unique—historical patterns may not repeat. Over-analysis can paralyze action.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Herbert Simon's bounded rationality theory ('Administrative Behavior', 1947) and behavioral economics. Draws on optimization theory, cognitive psychology on decision fatigue, and evolutionary psychology on good enough heuristics. Connects to Kahneman's System 1/2 framework, nudge theory, and organizational decision-making research.

### Negative-Sum Game · #1312 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** An interaction where total value is destroyed—all parties collectively end up with less than they started. War, litigation that costs more than the dispute, price wars that destroy industry margins, and arms races that consume resources without changing relative position are all negative-sum games. Unlike zero-sum (redistribution) or positive-sum (value creation), negative-sum games leave everyone poorer. They typically emerge from escalation dynamics, mutual distrust, or the absence of coordination mechanisms.
- **How it helps:** Recognize when you're in a negative-sum dynamic—before it escalates to the point where exit costs exceed continuation costs. The warning signs: both parties are spending more to compete than the prize is worth; the competition itself is degrading the asset being competed over; retaliation spirals are escalating without resolution. The exit strategy: unilateral de-escalation (risky but can break the spiral), third-party mediation, or reframing the interaction as positive-sum.
- **When to use:** When competition is destroying more value than it's capturing; when escalation dynamics are consuming resources disproportionate to the stakes; when both parties in a conflict are getting worse off over time; when designing institutional structures to prevent destructive competition.
- **Unique value:** Adds a critical category beyond the zero-sum/positive-sum dichotomy. Many situations labeled 'competitive' are actually value-destroying for all participants. Naming the category makes it visible, which is the first step toward avoiding or exiting these dynamics.
- **Limits:** Sometimes participating in a negative-sum game is necessary to prevent a worse outcome (deterrence). Identifying a game as negative-sum requires calculating total costs across all parties, which may not be visible. The label can be used to pressure parties into unilateral concession. Some short-term negative-sum dynamics lead to long-term positive-sum outcomes (creative destruction).
- **Foundations:** Formal game theory (von Neumann & Morgenstern) classifying games by payoff structure. Negative-sum outcomes arise from transaction costs, conflict destruction, or value dissipation. Extends Nash equilibrium analysis to situations where aggregate welfare declines through strategic interaction.

### Win-Lose · #1313 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A framing in which one party's gain is the other's loss—a zero-sum game. While some situations are genuinely zero-sum (dividing a fixed pie, competitive markets with fixed demand), most real-world situations have potential for positive-sum outcomes that win-lose framing obscures. The danger of win-lose thinking isn't that it's always wrong—it's that it becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. When you approach a negotiation as win-lose, you behave competitively, which triggers competitive behavior in the other party, creating the zero-sum dynamic you assumed.
- **How it helps:** Before any negotiation or competitive interaction, ask: is this genuinely zero-sum, or am I imposing win-lose framing on a situation that could be positive-sum? Most negotiations have zones of mutual gain that adversarial framing prevents discovering. The test: could we expand the pie before dividing it? If the answer is 'maybe,' win-lose framing is costing you value. Reserve win-lose thinking for genuinely zero-sum situations and approach everything else as potential win-win.
- **When to use:** When entering negotiations and need to assess whether the situation is zero-sum or positive-sum; when competitive framing is preventing creative solutions; when a conflict seems intractable—reframing from win-lose to potential win-win often reveals new options; when designing incentive structures that pit parties against each other unnecessarily.
- **Unique value:** Exposes how framing determines strategy. In genuinely zero-sum games, competitive strategy is rational. But the most common error is treating positive-sum games as zero-sum, which destroys potential value for everyone. The model's value is in the diagnostic question: is this really zero-sum?
- **Limits:** Some situations genuinely are zero-sum—denying this is naive. In competitive markets, your gain often is your competitor's loss. Over-applying win-win thinking can lead to weak negotiation where you concede value unnecessarily. The skill is correctly identifying which situations are zero-sum and which aren't.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in zero-sum game theory (von Neumann & Morgenstern) and distributive bargaining theory. Draws on competitive strategy frameworks and resource-based economics where one party's gain requires another's equivalent loss. Foundational to negotiation research by Raiffa and Lax & Sebenius.
