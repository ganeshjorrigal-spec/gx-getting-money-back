### Narrative Identity (McAdams): The Story You Tell About Yourself · #290 · `llm-high` · identity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Dan McAdams's framework that personal identity is fundamentally NARRATIVE in structure—you are the story you tell about yourself, and that story determines meaning, motivation, and psychological health. Narrative identity integrates: the reconstructed past (where you've been), the perceived present (who you are now), and the imagined future (who you're becoming) into a coherent story. The story has: themes (agency, communion), a tone (optimistic, tragic, comedic), turning points (events that changed the story's direction), and a sense of generativity (what the story contributes beyond yourself). Psychological health correlates with NARRATIVE COHERENCE—the ability to construct a meaningful, integrated story—not with having experienced good events.
- **How it helps:** Examine and deliberately author your narrative identity. The practices: (1) Write your life story in chapters—what themes emerge? What turning points defined who you became? (2) Examine the TONE: is your narrative primarily redemptive (bad things led to growth) or contaminating (good things were ruined)? Redemptive narratives predict better mental health. (3) Identify narrative gaps: are there experiences that haven't been integrated into your story? These unintegrated experiences often produce unresolved psychological tension. (4) Author forward: what's the next chapter? Your imagined future shapes present behavior as much as your reconstructed past.
- **When to use:** When identity feels fragmented or incoherent; when past experiences need to be integrated into a meaningful narrative; when the story you tell about yourself is limiting rather than enabling; when understanding that identity IS narrative would enable deliberate authorship of self.
- **Unique value:** Establishes that identity isn't discovered but AUTHORED—you construct your self through the story you tell about your life. This makes narrative revision a legitimate tool for identity change: changing the story changes the self. People with redemptive narratives (finding growth in adversity) show measurably better psychological outcomes.
- **Limits:** Narrative coherence can be forced at the expense of truth. Not all cultures emphasize narrative identity equally. Some experiences resist narrativization. Narrative revision without genuine processing can be superficial. The relationship between narrative and wellbeing is correlational.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Dan McAdams drawing on narrative psychology, hermeneutics (Ricoeur), and identity theory. Roots in Erikson's life story model, Bruner's narrative cognition research, and personal construct theory (Kelly). Connected to Pennebaker's expressive writing research and meaning-making frameworks.

### Possible Selves Theory (Markus & Nurius) — Applied · #1493 · `llm-high` · identity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius' possible selves theory identifies three categories of future self-representations that powerfully motivate behavior: hoped-for selves (who you want to become), expected selves (who you think you will become), and feared selves (who you dread becoming). These possible selves function as cognitive bridges between current identity and future behavior. Research shows that possible selves are most motivating when they are vivid, specific, and balanced—having a hoped-for self paired with a corresponding feared self (e.g., 'successful author' paired with 'someone who never finished the book') produces stronger motivation than either alone. The theory also explains identity crises: when your possible selves become unclear, contradictory, or collapse (no remaining hoped-for selves seem achievable), motivation and well-being decline dramatically.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers, possible selves theory provides a concrete motivational technology. Use it to: (1) articulate your hoped-for, expected, and feared professional selves in vivid detail—specificity drives motivation, (2) pair each hope with a corresponding fear for maximum motivational force, (3) evaluate whether your daily actions are moving you toward hoped-for selves or away from feared selves—both are legitimate motivational strategies, (4) when motivation drops, check whether your possible selves have become vague or feel unachievable—revision may restore motivation, (5) seek environments that make diverse possible selves visible (mentors, diverse networks, exposure to.
- **When to use:** When motivation has declined and standard goal-setting isn't working. When you need to make long-term career decisions and want clarity about future identity. When coaching others through career development. When your feared self is more motivating than your hoped-for self and you want to understand why.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific mechanism linking future self-representations to present behavior. Identifies the paired hope-fear structure as maximally motivating—more actionable than generic 'vision' exercises. Explains motivation loss through possible selves collapse rather than just laziness or burnout.
- **Limits:** Possible selves are constrained by what you can imagine, which is constrained by your social context—the theory may underserve those with limited exposure to diverse role models. Fear-based motivation (feared selves) can produce anxiety rather than productive action. The theory emphasizes individual agency and may underemphasize structural barriers to achieving possible selves.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius's cognitive social psychology research on self-concept. Draws on goal theory, motivational psychology, expectancy-value theory, and possible selves' linkage to Bandura's self-efficacy and identity-based motivation research.

### Authentic Self vs. Performed Self · #1502 · `high` · identity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The tension between authentic self-expression and strategic self-presentation is one of the central challenges of modern identity. Erving Goffman's dramaturgical model showed that all social interaction involves performance—we manage impressions, present frontstage selves to audiences, and maintain backstage selves in private. The 'authenticity' movement pushes back, arguing that performance is exhausting and that genuine self-expression produces better relationships and well-being. The resolution lies in recognizing that the dichotomy is false: we are always performing, AND some performances are more aligned with our core values and experiences than others. 'Authenticity' isn't the absence of performance but the alignment between performance and internal experience.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers who constantly navigate professional self-presentation, this model resolves the authenticity paradox. Use it to: (1) stop pursuing an impossible 'no performance' ideal—all professional interaction involves impression management, (2) evaluate your performances for alignment with your values rather than elimination of all presentation, (3) identify where your professional persona diverges from your values and gradually close that gap, (4) use Kernis and Goldman's four components as a self-assessment: Am I aware of my real feelings? Am I honest about my qualities? Am I genuine in relationships? Am I acting in line with my values? (5) recognize that 'being yourself at.
- **When to use:** When professional self-presentation feels exhausting and you suspect inauthenticity. When 'be your authentic self' advice conflicts with legitimate professional role requirements. When evaluating whether organizational culture rewards or punishes authenticity. When navigating the tension between strategic career behavior and personal integrity.
- **Unique value:** Resolves the false dichotomy between 'always performing' and 'pure authenticity.' Provides an operationalized definition of authentic functioning with four measurable components. Offers a practical framework for professional self-presentation that maintains integrity.
- **Limits:** Authenticity is culturally constructed—what counts as 'real' varies across cultures. Marginalized groups may face genuine risks from authentic self-expression that the model doesn't fully address. The concept can be co-opted by organizations demanding 'authentic' behavior while punishing actual authenticity. Some people have harmful authentic impulses that should be managed, not expressed.
- **Foundations:** Draws on Goffman's dramaturgical sociology, Winnicott's true/false self distinction, and existential philosophy (Sartre's authenticity, Heidegger's ownmost existence). Integrates social identity theory and positive psychology research on authenticity as a wellbeing determinant.
