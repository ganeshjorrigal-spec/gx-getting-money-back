### Preferred Stock vs Common Stock · #215 · `high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The distinction between two classes of equity ownership with different rights: common stock provides voting rights and residual claim on earnings (dividends and appreciation) but is LAST in line during liquidation. Preferred stock provides priority in dividends and liquidation but typically has no voting rights and limited appreciation potential. As a mental model: the preferred/common distinction illustrates the fundamental tradeoff between SAFETY (preferred—higher priority, more predictable returns) and UPSIDE (common—lower priority but unlimited appreciation). In venture capital: investors take preferred stock to protect downside while founders hold common stock to capture upside—each accepting a different risk/reward profile.
- **How it helps:** Apply the preferred/common framework to any resource allocation with different risk/reward profiles. In career: a stable salary is 'preferred' (predictable, protected) while equity compensation is 'common' (risky but potentially much larger). In business: guaranteed contracts are preferred (reliable revenue) while speculative markets are common (uncertain but potentially transformative). The strategic question: how much of your portfolio should be 'preferred' (safe, predictable) vs. 'common' (risky, high-upside)? Life stage, risk tolerance, and financial cushion determine the optimal mix.
- **When to use:** When structuring deals that need to accommodate different risk preferences; when portfolio allocation needs to balance safety with upside; when understanding why different stakeholders in the same venture accept different terms; when the safety-vs-upside tradeoff needs explicit analysis.
- **Unique value:** Provides the specific structural mechanism for accommodating different risk appetites in the SAME investment: preferred holders accept limited upside for downside protection; common holders accept more risk for unlimited upside. This solves the problem of how parties with different risk profiles can participate in the same venture.
- **Limits:** The preferred/common distinction is a simplification of complex financial instruments. Preferred stock comes in many varieties with different terms. The metaphorical extension to non-financial contexts is approximate. In practice, terms are negotiated and vary widely.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in corporate finance theory, capital structure theory (Modigliani-Miller), and financial contracting. Connects to agency theory, risk-return tradeoffs in portfolio theory, and venture capital deal structure research.

### Money Scripts (Klontz): Unconscious Beliefs Driving Financial Behavior · #546 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Brad Klontz's financial psychology framework identifying four clusters of unconscious beliefs about money that drive financial behavior: (1) Money Avoidance ('money is bad,' 'rich people are greedy')—produces self-sabotage, under-earning, and avoidance of financial management. (2) Money Worship ('more money will solve everything')—produces workaholism, overspending, and the belief that happiness is just one more raise away. (3) Money Status ('my worth equals my net worth')—produces conspicuous consumption, financial risk-taking for status, and shame about financial position. (4) Money Vigilance ('save everything, trust no one with money')—produces excessive frugality, financial anxiety, and difficulty enjoying earned wealth. Money scripts are formed in childhood and operate below conscious awareness.
- **How it helps:** Identify your dominant money scripts through self-examination: which beliefs about money feel TRUE rather than CHOSEN? The diagnostic: 'What did my family teach me about money—explicitly and implicitly?' Common inherited scripts: 'we can't afford that' (avoidance), 'money shows who's winning' (status), 'you have to work harder' (worship), 'never trust anyone with your money' (vigilance). Once identified: test each script against evidence. Is this belief helping or hindering your financial goals? The practice: consciously replace limiting scripts with evidence-based financial principles while honoring the protective function the original script served.
- **When to use:** When financial behavior doesn't match financial knowledge (you know better but can't act on it); when money-related anxiety, guilt, or shame is driving decisions; when inherited beliefs about money are limiting financial effectiveness; when understanding the psychological roots of financial behavior would enable better outcomes.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific psychological mechanism behind the gap between financial knowledge and financial behavior: unconscious money scripts formed in childhood that override rational financial analysis. This explains why financial education alone doesn't change behavior—the scripts must be identified and consciously updated.
- **Limits:** Not all financial behavior is script-driven. Money scripts are a simplified model of complex psychological dynamics. Changing deep-seated beliefs requires more than intellectual understanding. Professional support may be needed for entrenched scripts. Cultural context shapes which scripts are prevalent.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in clinical psychology (Klontz Money Script Inventory), schema therapy (Young), and psychoanalytic object relations theory. Draws on intergenerational transmission of financial behavior research, social learning theory (Bandura), and behavioral economics of money illusion. Money scripts operate as cognitive schemas activated by financial contexts.

### Perfect Competition · #1325 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A theoretical market structure with many buyers and sellers, identical products, perfect information, and zero entry barriers—where no individual firm can influence the market price. In perfect competition, economic profit is zero in the long run because any positive profit attracts new entrants who compete it away. While no real market is perfectly competitive, the model explains a critical strategic truth: in the absence of differentiation, barriers, or information asymmetry, profits converge to zero. Competitive strategy is essentially the art of avoiding perfect competition.
- **How it helps:** If your business has no meaningful differentiation, low switching costs, easy entry for competitors, and transparent pricing, you're approaching perfect competition—and your margins will reflect it. The strategic imperative: create conditions that differ from perfect competition. Moats (brands, network effects, patents, switching costs) all work by breaking one of perfect competition's assumptions. When analyzing any market, ask: which assumption of perfect competition is violated here, and for how long?
- **When to use:** When evaluating whether a market offers sustainable profit potential; when margins are compressing and you're wondering why; when designing strategy—every strategic action is an attempt to move away from perfect competition; when assessing competitive moats—each moat violates one or more assumptions of perfect competition.
- **Unique value:** Provides the theoretical benchmark against which all strategic advantage is defined. If you can't articulate which assumption of perfect competition your business violates, you don't have a competitive advantage. This makes the abstract concept immediately actionable for strategy.
- **Limits:** No real market is perfectly competitive, so the model is a theoretical reference point rather than a description of reality. The model assumes rational actors and ignores transaction costs, which are significant in every real market. Some businesses (commodities) operate close to perfect competition and survive through operational efficiency rather than strategic differentiation.
- **Foundations:** Central model of neoclassical microeconomic theory, articulated by Marshall and later formalized in general equilibrium theory (Walras, Arrow-Debreu). Draws on price theory, welfare economics, and industrial organization. Serves as the benchmark competitive standard against which real markets are evaluated.

### Oligopoly · #1326 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A market structure dominated by a small number of large firms whose strategic decisions are interdependent—each firm must consider rivals' likely responses when making pricing, investment, or marketing decisions. Unlike monopoly (one firm) or perfect competition (many firms), oligopoly strategy is inherently game-theoretic. Airlines, telecom, auto manufacturing, and big tech are all oligopolies. The key dynamic: firms can either compete aggressively (price wars that destroy everyone's margins) or tacitly coordinate (maintaining prices that benefit all incumbents at consumers' expense).
- **How it helps:** If you're in an oligopoly (most mature industries), your strategy must account for competitive response. Price cuts that make sense in isolation can trigger price wars that destroy industry profitability. Product innovations get copied quickly. The strategic options are: compete on non-price dimensions (differentiation), signal cooperation (price leadership), or disrupt the oligopoly structure entirely. Understanding which game you're playing determines which moves make sense.
- **When to use:** When operating in any market with 2-6 dominant players; when considering pricing changes that rivals will see and respond to; when evaluating whether to compete or differentiate; when assessing industry profitability dynamics and why margins stabilize or collapse.
- **Unique value:** Reveals why competitive strategy in concentrated markets differs fundamentally from strategy in fragmented markets. In oligopoly, your optimal move depends on your competitor's optimal move, which depends on your optimal move—creating strategic interdependence that requires game-theoretic thinking rather than simple optimization.
- **Limits:** Tacit coordination can become illegal collusion. Oligopoly analysis assumes rational actors, but real firms make emotional and political decisions. Industry structure changes—oligopolies can be disrupted by new entrants with different business models. The number of firms alone doesn't determine market dynamics; differentiation, capacity, and entry barriers matter too.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in industrial organization economics and Cournot's oligopoly model (1838). Draws on game theory (Nash equilibrium applied to strategic firm interaction), Chamberlin's theory of monopolistic competition, Bain's structure-conduct-performance paradigm, and the Herfindahl-Hirschman concentration index.

### Monopsony · #1327 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A market with a single buyer (or dominant buyer) who has outsized power to dictate terms to sellers. The mirror image of monopoly: where a monopolist controls supply and raises prices, a monopsonist controls demand and suppresses prices. Walmart's relationship with small suppliers, Amazon's relationship with third-party sellers, and large employers in small towns are all monopsony dynamics. The result: suppliers are forced to accept unfavorable terms because there's no alternative buyer.
- **How it helps:** If you're selling to one dominant customer who represents more than 30-40% of your revenue, you're in a monopsony trap. The buyer's negotiating power increases with your dependency. The strategic response: diversify your customer base before the dominant buyer realizes their leverage, or become so essential to the buyer that the relationship shifts from extractive to mutual. For investors and strategists: identify monopsony dynamics in markets you're entering—they determine who captures value in the chain.
- **When to use:** When evaluating whether a customer, platform, or buyer has disproportionate power over your business; when assessing market structure and value chain dynamics; when a supplier consistently faces margin pressure from a dominant buyer; when building strategy in markets with concentrated buying power.
- **Unique value:** The complement to monopoly analysis that most business strategy ignores. While everyone watches for supply-side market power, demand-side market power (monopsony) is equally important and less discussed. It explains why small suppliers in platform-dominated markets face relentless margin pressure despite providing essential value.
- **Limits:** Pure monopsony is rare—most markets have some buyer competition. Monopsony power can be offset by supplier differentiation or switching costs. Regulation sometimes addresses monopsony (antitrust, minimum wage), but enforcement is uneven. The model describes market structure but doesn't prescribe the optimal response—that depends on specific bargaining dynamics.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in labor economics and Joan Robinson's monopsony theory (1933). Draws on market power analysis from industrial organization, Pigou's welfare economics, and modern labor market research on employer concentration. Related to bilateral monopoly theory and bargaining frameworks in economics.

### Club Goods · #1337 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Goods that are excludable (you can prevent non-payers from accessing them) but non-rivalrous (one person's consumption doesn't diminish another's). Movie theaters, subscription services, private golf courses, and gated online communities are all club goods. The economic model underlying every membership business: charge for access to something that doesn't deplete with use. Club goods exist between pure private goods (excludable and rivalrous) and public goods (non-excludable and non-rivalrous).
- **How it helps:** If you're building a membership, subscription, or community business, you're selling a club good. The key strategic questions: How do you make it excludable (paywall, membership requirements)? How do you prevent it from becoming rivalrous (server capacity, community quality as it scales)? The most successful club goods maintain quality at scale—Netflix at 200 million subscribers is still non-rivalrous because streaming one show doesn't prevent another person from streaming.
- **When to use:** When designing subscription or membership business models; when evaluating whether a product is naturally a club good, private good, or public good; when pricing excludable services; when community or content businesses face scaling decisions.
- **Unique value:** Provides precise economic language for 'membership business.' The two dimensions—excludability and rivalry—reveal the structural economics of any product. Understanding which quadrant your product occupies determines pricing strategy, scaling dynamics, and competitive moat.
- **Limits:** Many club goods become partially rivalrous at scale (crowded parks, slow servers, diluted community quality). The excludability mechanism has costs—DRM, paywalls, authentication—that create friction. Digital goods blur the boundaries because copying is essentially free, making excludability artificial rather than natural.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Buchanan's public goods theory and the economics of excludability and rivalry. Draws on Olson's Logic of Collective Action, institutional economics of common-pool resources (Ostrom), and the Samuelson public goods framework. Sits between pure public goods and private goods in the excludability-rivalry taxonomy.

### Frugality vs. Earning Power Tradeoff · #1594 · `high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Two paths to surplus: reducing expenses (frugality) and increasing income (earning power). Frugality has a hard floor but offers immediate, controllable results. Earning power has no ceiling but requires investment and time. The optimal strategy changes with income: at low incomes, frugality provides highest marginal return. At high incomes, further frugality yields diminishing returns and earning power investments dominate. Most advice is stuck at one extreme. The optimal strategy dynamically shifts: frugality-first at lower incomes, earning-power-first at higher incomes, always maintaining both.
- **How it helps:** For young professionals on modest incomes, this validates frugality-first while providing a transition point: once major expenses are optimized (housing, transport, food), shift to earning power (skills, career moves, negotiation). For high earners, it explains why further cutting feels pointless—because it is, relative to earning power. The framework: calculate your frugality-to-earning effort ratio, compare to income level, rebalance. At $50K, 70% frugality focus makes sense. At $200K, 70% earning power focus makes sense.
- **When to use:** When deciding whether to cut expenses or invest in earning more. When frugality efforts produce diminishing returns. When high income hasn't translated to wealth. When creating a financial strategy and deciding where to focus limited energy.
- **Unique value:** Provides a dynamic framework shifting between frugality and earning power based on income level, rather than dogmatically advocating one approach. Names the specific income inflection point where the optimal balance shifts between the two strategies.
- **Limits:** The transition point is hard to identify precisely. Some people are psychologically suited to one approach regardless. Earning power isn't fully controllable. The model can justify excessive spending at high incomes.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in microeconomics of diminishing marginal returns, behavioral economics of consumption (Schor, Veblen), and personal finance theory. Human capital economics (Becker) provides the framework for returns on earning power investment. The dynamic threshold concept draws from portfolio theory rebalancing. Labor economics of wage negotiation and career capital (Newport) supplements the model.
