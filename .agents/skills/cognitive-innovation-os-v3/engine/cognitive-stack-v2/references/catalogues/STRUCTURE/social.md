### Dunbar's Number Layers Applied to Friendship · #1438 · `high` · social · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Robin Dunbar's research identified nested social layers based on emotional closeness and interaction frequency: 5 (intimate support group — people you'd call in a crisis at 3am), 15 (sympathy group — close friends whose death would devastate you), 50 (close friends — people you'd invite to a large dinner party), 150 (casual friends — people you maintain active relationships with), 500 (acquaintances — people you recognize and can have a conversation with), and 1500 (people you can recognize by name). Each layer requires different investment: the inner 5 need weekly contact, the 15 need monthly, the 50 need quarterly. The layers are also constrained by cognitive capacity — maintaining each relationship requires mental bandwidth for tracking preferences, history, emotional states, and reciprocity. The critical insight for adults is that these layers are zero-sum at any given time: adding someone to your inner 5 typically means someone else moves outward. Active management of these layers, rather than passive drift, is the difference between deliberate social architecture and accidental isolation.
- **How it helps:** Map your current social layers: Who are your 5? Your 15? Your 50? Most knowledge workers discover gaps — perhaps a robust 50 but a depleted 5, or vice versa. Each layer serves different functions: the 5 provide deep emotional support, the 15 offer reliable companionship, the 50 create community, the 150 provide diverse perspective and opportunity. Design your social investment accordingly: if your inner 5 is weak, no amount of networking at the 500 level will compensate. Schedule regular contact with each layer at appropriate frequency. The model also explains why social media friends (typically 500+ layer) don't reduce loneliness — they're too distant to serve the functions that the 5-15 layer provides.
- **When to use:** When feeling socially disconnected despite having many acquaintances. When deciding how to allocate limited social time. When a friendship feels like it's fading (has the person moved to a different layer?). When designing your social life deliberately rather than letting it drift. When evaluating the health of your social portfolio.
- **Unique value:** Provides specific numbers and frequencies for each social layer, converting vague advice to 'maintain friendships' into a concrete audit and investment plan. The zero-sum constraint is particularly actionable: adding relationships requires acknowledging which ones will receive less investment.
- **Limits:** Dunbar's numbers are averages with significant individual variation. Introverts may have smaller optimal layers; extraverts larger. Cultural differences in friendship expectations affect layer sizes. Digital communication may shift layer boundaries. The model can feel cold if applied too mechanically.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Robin Dunbar's neocortical constraint hypothesis from evolutionary anthropology and primatology. Draws on social neuroscience, cognitive anthropology, and sociological network theory to explain why human social group sizes cluster at consistent numerical layers.
- **Recognition cue:** "How big is this group, and what coordination overhead does that imply?"
- **Worked example:** Military companies, hunter-gatherer bands, Hutterite community splits, startup org design

### Bridge vs. Bond Social Capital · #1452 · `high` · social · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Robert Putnam's distinction between bridging and bonding social capital describes two fundamentally different types of social connection. Bonding social capital connects people who are similar — same identity, same values, same social group — and provides strong in-group solidarity, emotional support, and mutual aid. Bridging social capital connects people across different groups — different identities, industries, worldviews — and provides access to diverse information, novel opportunities, and broader perspective. Both are essential but serve different functions: bonding capital is the social equivalent of a savings account (reliable, deep, but limited in scope), while bridging capital is the social equivalent of an investment portfolio (diverse, generative, but requiring more maintenance). Communities heavy on bonding but light on bridging become insular, parochial, and resistant to change. Communities heavy on bridging but light on bonding lack the deep trust needed for collective action during crisis.
- **How it helps:** Audit your social network: Is it mostly bonding (people like you) or mostly bridging (people different from you)? Most knowledge workers' networks are unbalanced toward bonding — their friends share their education, industry, political views, and lifestyle. To add bridging capital: join communities outside your professional identity, volunteer with different demographic groups, attend events in adjacent industries, or simply diversify your media diet. For organizations, this framework explains why homogeneous teams feel comfortable but innovate less (bonding without bridging), while maximally diverse teams innovate more but may struggle with cohesion (bridging without bonding). The optimal is both.
- **When to use:** When your social network feels like an echo chamber. When evaluating community health (is it all bonding or all bridging?). When designing teams that need both cohesion and innovation. When assessing whether a new community investment will add bonding or bridging capital to your portfolio.
- **Unique value:** Provides specific language for two distinct social resources that are often conflated under 'community.' The savings/investment metaphor makes the functional difference immediately clear and helps diagnose which type your network needs more of.
- **Limits:** The bonding/bridging distinction is not always clean — many relationships provide both. Power dynamics mean that bridging capital benefits some groups more than others. Bridging across genuine value differences is harder than the model implies. The framework can undervalue bonding capital in communities that need solidarity more than diversity.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Robert Putnam's distinction between bridging and bonding social capital in civic sociology. Draws on social network theory (Granovetter, Burt), sociological research on community cohesion, and Coleman's work on social capital functions within closed networks.

### Intimacy-Passion-Commitment Triangle (Sternberg) · #1516 · `llm-high` · social · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Robert Sternberg's Triangular Theory of Love identifies three components—intimacy (emotional closeness, bonding, connectedness), passion (physical attraction, sexual desire, romantic arousal), and commitment (decision to love and maintain the relationship)—that combine in different proportions to produce seven types of love: liking (intimacy only), infatuation (passion only), empty love (commitment only), romantic love (intimacy + passion), companionate love (intimacy + commitment), fatuous love (passion + commitment), and consummate love (all three). The practical insight is that these components follow different temporal trajectories in relationships: passion peaks early and declines, intimacy grows gradually, and commitment develops in steps.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers who think analytically about relationships, Sternberg's model provides a diagnostic framework. Use it to: (1) assess which component is depleted in a struggling relationship rather than declaring the whole relationship broken, (2) understand that the natural decline of passion doesn't mean the relationship is failing—the composition of love is shifting, not dying, (3) invest in the component that's most depleted: if intimacy is low, prioritize emotional connection; if passion is low, prioritize novelty and desire; if commitment is low, clarify and recommit, (4) apply the framework to professional passion: enthusiasm for a role (passion) may decline while expertise.
- **When to use:** When relationship dissatisfaction needs diagnosis rather than wholesale relationship evaluation. When the natural evolution of love's composition is being misinterpreted as love's death. When prioritizing relationship investments and needing a framework for where to focus. When professional engagement shifts and you need to understand which dimension has changed.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific three-component taxonomy that enables precise diagnosis of what's missing. Explains the temporal trajectory of love's components, preventing premature relationship abandonment. Bridges romantic and non-romantic engagement frameworks.
- **Limits:** The three-component model may oversimplify the complexity of love. Cultural concepts of love may not map cleanly onto intimacy, passion, and commitment. The model doesn't account for power dynamics, attachment style, or external stressors. Some critics argue the components aren't as separable as the model implies.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Robert Sternberg's triangular theory of love in social psychology. Draws on attachment theory, relationship science research (Gottman), evolutionary psychology, and philosophical traditions distinguishing eros, philia, and pragma as love's component dimensions.
