### Dealbreaker vs. Tradeoff Distinction · #1674 · `high` · negotiation · CLAIM
- **What it is:** In every negotiation, there are dealbreakers (non-negotiable requirements that must be met for any deal to be possible) and tradeoffs (issues where you have flexibility and can exchange concessions). Confusing these categories is one of the most common negotiation errors. Treating a tradeoff as a dealbreaker makes you unnecessarily rigid, potentially killing deals that would have been valuable. Treating a dealbreaker as a tradeoff means you'll end up in agreements that don't actually work. Before negotiating, honestly categorize every issue: is this truly something I cannot accept under any circumstances, or is this something I'd prefer but could trade away for sufficient compensation? Most people have far fewer genuine dealbreakers than they think—and the discipline of identifying real dealbreakers prevents you from losing them in the heat of negotiation.
- **How it helps:** Before every negotiation, write two lists: absolute dealbreakers (typically 1-3 items) and tradeoffs (everything else, ranked by priority). For your dealbreakers, define them precisely and commit to walking away if they're not met. For your tradeoffs, determine your preferences and your acceptable range. During negotiation, explicitly flag your dealbreakers so the other party doesn't waste time trying to negotiate them: 'Payment terms of net 30 are a firm requirement; I'm flexible on everything else.' This clarity actually makes negotiation easier because it focuses both parties on the issues where agreement is possible.
- **When to use:** During negotiation preparation, to separate what's truly non-negotiable from what's merely important. When negotiations are stalled and you need to identify which issues are actually blocking agreement. When the other party presents many demands and you need to distinguish their real priorities from negotiation positions.
- **Unique value:** Provides a simple but powerful categorization that prevents the two most common negotiation errors: unnecessary rigidity (treating preferences as requirements) and dangerous flexibility (trading away genuine requirements for concessions).
- **Limits:** Dealbreakers can change with circumstances—what seemed non-negotiable becomes flexible when the alternative is no deal at all. Revealing your dealbreakers gives the other party information they can exploit. Some issues are genuinely ambiguous between dealbreaker and tradeoff.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in multi-attribute utility theory (MAUT), decision analysis (Raiffa, Keeney), and cognitive psychology of value hierarchies. Draws from Maslow's needs hierarchy, Schwartz's value theory, and behavioral decision research on preference elicitation and threshold effects in judgment.
