### Circle of Competence · #253 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Each person has areas where they have genuine, earned knowledge and expertise—their circle of competence—and vast areas where they don't. The key is knowing the boundaries of your circle: operating inside it gives you an edge; operating outside it without recognizing it leads to costly errors. Building and maintaining a circle requires years of learning and practice, honest feedback, and willingness to stay within bounds.
- **How it helps:** Prevents entrepreneurs from overextending into domains they don't understand. Helps knowledge workers know when to defer to others vs. trust their own judgment. Warren Buffett attributes much of Berkshire's success to staying within their circle and saying 'no' to opportunities outside it.
- **When to use:** Before entering new markets, making investments, or offering opinions on unfamiliar subjects. When deciding whether to build in-house vs. hire expertise. When evaluating your own confidence on a topic.
- **Unique value:** Uniquely combines intellectual humility with practical competitive advantage. Knowing what you don't know is as valuable as knowing what you do.
- **Limits:** Circles can shrink if not maintained—expertise decays. Can become an excuse to never learn new things. The boundary is often fuzzy, not a clean line. Dunning-Kruger effect makes it hard to self-assess accurately.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in epistemology and the philosophy of knowledge, particularly Socratic awareness of ignorance. Draws on cognitive psychology (metacognition), organizational learning theory (Argyris), and Buffett/Munger value investing philosophy. Connected to Dunning-Kruger research and expertise studies.

### Two-by-Two Matrix · #275 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A framework that maps options, ideas, or items along two independent dimensions, creating four quadrants that reveal strategic categories. The Eisenhower Matrix (urgent × important), BCG Matrix (market share × growth), and risk-reward plots are all two-by-two matrices. The power is in making hidden trade-offs visible: by forcing you to evaluate on two dimensions simultaneously, the matrix reveals categories (high importance/low urgency) that single-dimension thinking misses entirely.
- **How it helps:** When facing a complex decision with multiple factors, identify the two most important dimensions and plot your options. The magic happens in the surprising quadrants—the combinations you didn't initially consider. High-urgency, low-importance tasks (Eisenhower's 'delegate' quadrant) are invisible without the matrix. High-risk, high-reward opportunities look different from high-risk, low-reward traps—but only when you plot both dimensions. The discipline: choose the RIGHT two dimensions. Bad axes produce useless matrices.
- **When to use:** When you need to prioritize across multiple options with competing dimensions; when a decision involves trade-offs between two important factors; when you want to create a shared visual framework for team decision-making; when the existing categorization feels one-dimensional.
- **Unique value:** The most compact strategic framework: two dimensions, four quadrants, instant insight. No other tool generates as much analytical clarity per minute of effort. The constraint of exactly two dimensions forces you to identify what actually matters most—a valuable discipline in itself.
- **Limits:** Most real situations involve more than two dimensions—the matrix forces a simplification that may miss critical factors. The choice of axes is subjective and dramatically affects conclusions. The four-quadrant structure can create false categorical boundaries in what's actually a continuous space. People tend to over-rely on the matrix once drawn, treating it as more definitive than it is.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in decision matrix methodology in management science and the BCG matrix tradition in strategy consulting. Draws on Cartesian coordinate systems, set theory, and multivariate analysis. Connected to Johari Window psychology, Ansoff Matrix in strategic management, and data visualization theory.

### Flow Model (Csikszentmihalyi) · #753 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Csikszentmihalyi's psychological model identifying the conditions for optimal experience: flow occurs when challenge level closely matches skill level, goals are clear, feedback is immediate, and the activity demands full concentration. Below the challenge-skill match: boredom (too easy) or apathy (low challenge, low skill). Above it: anxiety (too hard) or arousal (high challenge, slightly below skill). The flow channel is the narrow band where challenge and skill are both high and closely matched—producing the state of complete absorption where time distorts, self-consciousness dissolves, and performance peaks.
- **How it helps:** Engineer flow conditions deliberately: (1) Set clear goals for each work session so you know exactly what 'progress' looks like. (2) Match difficulty to your current skill—stretch but don't overwhelm. (3) Ensure immediate feedback so you know whether your actions are working. (4) Eliminate distractions that break concentration. For teams: design work so each person operates in their flow channel—not bored, not overwhelmed. Assignments slightly above current skill level produce both flow and growth. The biggest flow killer in modern work: fragmented attention from meetings, notifications, and context-switching.
- **When to use:** When productivity is low despite adequate time and talent; when work feels either boring or overwhelming (both indicate challenge-skill mismatch); when designing work environments, assignments, or learning progressions; when building personal practices for peak performance.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the precise conditions for peak human performance with enough specificity to engineer them. Unlike vague advice to 'do what you love,' the flow model specifies four measurable conditions (challenge-skill match, clear goals, immediate feedback, deep concentration) that can be deliberately created.
- **Limits:** Flow isn't appropriate for all work—some tasks require deliberate analytical thinking, not absorption. Flow can be addictive, leading people to neglect important but non-flow activities. The challenge-skill calibration requires self-awareness that's hard to develop. Organizational conditions (interruptions, meeting culture) can make flow structurally impossible regardless of individual effort.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1975, 1990). Rooted in humanistic psychology, optimal experience theory, and positive psychology. Connected to self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan), attention research in cognitive psychology, and performance science on challenge-skill balance.

### Johari Window · #779 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A framework mapping self-knowledge across two dimensions—what you know about yourself and what others know about you—creating four quadrants: Open (known to self and others), Blind Spot (known to others but not to self), Hidden (known to self but not to others), and Unknown (known to neither). Personal and professional growth involves expanding the Open quadrant by: seeking feedback (reducing blind spots), sharing authentically (reducing hidden area), and self-discovery through new experiences (reducing the unknown). The Johari Window reveals that growth requires vulnerability—exposing yourself to feedback and sharing what you've hidden.
- **How it helps:** Use the Johari Window to guide personal development and team building. To reduce blind spots: actively solicit honest feedback from people who see you in action—and make it safe for them to be candid. To reduce the hidden area: share more of your thinking, reasoning, and concerns with teammates—vulnerability builds trust and improves collaboration. To reduce the unknown: pursue novel experiences that reveal aspects of yourself you haven't discovered. In teams: the collective Open quadrant determines how effectively the team communicates; expanding it is the single most effective team development practice.
- **When to use:** When personal development feels stuck (blind spots may be limiting growth); when team communication and trust need improvement; when feedback isn't flowing despite stated openness; when designing personal or team development programs.
- **Unique value:** Maps the specific relationship between self-awareness, others' awareness, and the growth that comes from aligning them. Unlike abstract advice to 'be more self-aware,' the Johari Window identifies the four distinct zones and the specific practice needed to expand each one: feedback for blind spots, disclosure for hidden areas, exploration for unknowns.
- **Limits:** People may not give honest feedback even when asked. Self-disclosure has real social risks in competitive environments. The 'Unknown' quadrant is by definition hard to explore systematically. Cultural differences affect norms around feedback and disclosure. The model is better as a conceptual framework than a precise tool.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Luft and Ingham (1955). Rooted in humanistic psychology (Rogers) and interpersonal process theory. Connected to self-disclosure research, Johari's framework in organizational development, feedback theory, and social psychology on meta-awareness and self-other perception gaps.
- **Recognition cue:** "Which quadrant does this trait live in for me?"
- **Worked example:** Self-awareness work, feedback design, executive coaching

### Attachment Theory (Bowlby/Ainsworth) · #876 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The developmental psychology framework showing that early relationships with caregivers create internal 'working models' of relationships that persist into adulthood—shaping how people approach intimacy, trust, conflict, and dependency. Secure attachment (caregiver was consistently responsive) produces adults comfortable with intimacy and independence. Anxious attachment (caregiver was inconsistently responsive) produces adults who crave closeness but fear abandonment. Avoidant attachment (caregiver was emotionally unavailable) produces adults who value independence and suppress emotional needs. These patterns are remarkably stable and predict relationship behavior decades later.
- **How it helps:** Understand your own attachment style and those of key people in your life and work. In relationships: attachment style predicts conflict patterns better than the content of disagreements. Anxious partners escalate to get reassurance; avoidant partners withdraw to manage overwhelm—creating the classic pursue-withdraw cycle. In leadership: attachment style affects how you handle dependency (team members depending on you) and how you respond to criticism. Self-awareness is the intervention: knowing your pattern doesn't eliminate it but creates space between trigger and response.
- **When to use:** When relationship patterns repeat despite different partners or contexts; when leadership style creates predictable but dysfunctional team dynamics; when conflict escalation follows the same script regardless of content; when designing team environments that need to account for different relationship orientations.
- **Unique value:** Provides the most empirically validated framework for understanding why people relate the way they do—tracing adult behavior to specific early relationship patterns. Unlike personality models that describe WHAT people do, attachment theory explains WHY (the internal working model) and predicts WHEN (the triggering conditions).
- **Limits:** Attachment styles aren't deterministic—people can develop 'earned security' through later relationships and therapy. The categories are simplifications of continuous variation. Cultural context shapes attachment norms. Over-pathologizing insecure attachment can create identity limitations. The theory focuses on early relationships and may underweight adult experiences.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Bowlby's ethological attachment theory drawing on Lorenz and Harlow, and Ainsworth's Strange Situation empirical work. Draws on object relations psychoanalytic theory (Winnicott, Klein), developmental neuroscience, and evolutionary psychology. Main and colleagues extended it to adult attachment representations.

### Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs · #879 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Abraham Maslow's motivational theory that human needs are arranged in a hierarchy: physiological needs (food, water, shelter) → safety needs (security, stability) → belonging needs (love, community) → esteem needs (respect, achievement) → self-actualization (reaching full potential). Lower-level needs must be sufficiently satisfied before higher-level needs become motivating. A hungry person doesn't care about self-actualization; a person with unmet safety needs doesn't prioritize esteem. Applied to management: compensation satisfies lower needs; once adequate, further compensation has diminishing motivational returns, and higher needs (belonging, purpose, growth) become the primary motivators.
- **How it helps:** Diagnose which level of the hierarchy is currently unmet for yourself, your team, or your customers—then address THAT level. If basic security needs (job stability, financial adequacy) are unmet, purpose-driven motivation will fail. If belonging needs are unmet (isolated, excluded), achievement goals will feel hollow. The most common leadership error: trying to motivate with higher-level rewards (purpose, growth) while lower-level needs (fair pay, job security, belonging) are unmet. Meet lower needs first. Then: once lower needs are met, don't keep investing there—move up the hierarchy to belonging, purpose, and growth.
- **When to use:** When motivation strategies aren't working (you may be targeting the wrong level); when compensation increases aren't increasing motivation (lower needs are already met—shift to higher needs); when new employees need different motivational approaches than veteran employees; when designing organizational culture and need to understand the motivational hierarchy.
- **Unique value:** Provides the diagnostic hierarchy for motivation: always start by identifying which level is currently unmet. The model's greatest practical value isn't the specific five levels—it's the principle that unmet lower needs block the motivational power of higher needs. Address needs in order, not in whichever order seems most appealing.
- **Limits:** The strict hierarchy has been challenged—people can pursue self-actualization while lower needs are partially unmet. The five levels are somewhat arbitrary. Cultural variation affects which needs are primary. The theory lacks strong empirical support for the rigid hierarchy. Individual variation is significant—some people are strongly motivated by esteem even when safety is threatened.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in humanistic psychology (Rogers, Goldstein) and Maslow's synthesis of clinical observation and motivational theory. Draws on evolutionary psychology's threat-detection priority systems and existentialist philosophy. Critiqued and refined through cross-cultural psychology and self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan).
- **Recognition cue:** "Which need-level is this person operating at right now?"
- **Worked example:** Crisis response (safety first), marketing positioning, employee motivation design
