### Sun Tzu's Terrain Analysis (Nine Situations) · #1841 · `llm-high` · cross-cultural · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Sun Tzu identified nine strategic terrains, each demanding different behavior. 'Dispersive ground' (own territory) — don't fight. 'Frontier ground' — keep moving. 'Key ground' (valuable to both sides) — race to occupy first. 'Intersecting ground' (junction of routes) — form alliances. 'Serious ground' (deep in enemy territory) — secure supply lines. 'Desperate ground' (fight or die) — commit totally. The genius: strategy should be determined by terrain, not by desire. Reading terrain correctly matters more than having a plan.
- **How it helps:** Map business situations to terrain types. A startup in an incumbent-dominated market is on 'serious ground' — secure resources, avoid pitched battles. A company being disrupted is on 'desperate ground' — half-measures fail. A product at the intersection of multiple markets occupies 'intersecting ground' — partnerships are essential. The framework prevents applying wrong strategies to wrong situations.
- **When to use:** When entering a new competitive situation and needing to determine strategic posture. When a strategy isn't working and you suspect you've misread the situation. When choosing between aggressive and conservative approaches. When advising others on handling their competitive position.
- **Unique value:** The oldest systematic taxonomy of strategic situations, forcing situation-specific rather than one-size-fits-all strategy. The nine terrains map well to modern business contexts. Unlike generic frameworks, it prescribes different behaviors for different positions.
- **Limits:** Military terrain metaphors don't perfectly translate to business. Categories may oversimplify complex situations blending multiple terrain types. Correct classification requires judgment the model cannot teach. Assumes competitive rather than collaborative contexts.
- **Foundations:** Founded in classical Chinese military philosophy (Sun Tzu), strategic analysis, and environmental assessment theory. Draws on terrain analysis in military doctrine, situational awareness research, SWOT analysis predecessors, and adaptive strategy theory in management.

### Elder Council Governance · #1918 · `llm-high` · cross-cultural · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Elder council governance, practiced across African, Indigenous American, Pacific Island, and Aboriginal Australian societies, vests primary decision-making authority in councils of elders chosen for their accumulated wisdom, demonstrated character, and long service to the community. Unlike gerontocracy (rule by the old simply because they are old), elder councils select members through demonstrated qualities: sound judgment in past crises, fairness in disputes, deep knowledge of community history and relationships, and proven commitment to collective welfare over personal advantage. The Haudenosaunee Clan Mothers chose and could depose chiefs; the Xhosa ibandla council deliberated until consensus; the Maasai council of elders (enkiguena) governed through moral authority rather than coercive power. Elder councils typically operated through consensus rather than majority vote, with the understanding that wisdom requires time and that decisions affecting the community should not be rushed.
- **How it helps:** Elder council governance offers a model for decision-making that values accumulated wisdom, institutional memory, and demonstrated character -- qualities that modern organizations systematically undervalue in favor of speed, innovation, and youth. The distinction between gerontocracy (power by age) and elder governance (power by demonstrated wisdom and service) is crucial: the model does not argue that age itself confers authority but that certain kinds of judgment -- pattern recognition across decades, understanding of human nature, knowledge of what has been tried before -- require extensive experience. For organizations, elder councils suggest that advisory boards, mentorship structures, and succession planning should prioritize demonstrated wisdom over credentials or seniority.
- **When to use:** When organizations are making the same mistakes repeatedly due to lack of institutional memory. When designing advisory or governance structures that need to value wisdom alongside innovation. When succession planning and mentorship need a clearer philosophical framework. When rapid decision-making has produced poor outcomes and slower, more deliberative governance is needed.
- **Unique value:** Distinguishes wisdom-based governance from mere seniority, providing criteria for what constitutes genuine elder authority. The consensus requirement ensures that decisions reflect collective wisdom rather than individual power. The model's cross-cultural prevalence suggests it addresses a universal governance need.
- **Limits:** Elder councils can become conservative bodies that resist necessary change. The selection criteria for 'wisdom' are subjective and can entrench existing power structures. Gender exclusion was common in many historical elder councils. Consensus requirements can produce gridlock on urgent matters.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in African governance traditions, anthropology of leadership, and gerontology. Connects to virtue ethics, deliberative democracy theory, and organizational behavior research on wisdom. Draws on comparative politics scholarship on consensus-based governance systems and traditional authority structures.

### Honne and Tatemae (Private vs. Public Self) · #1937 · `high` · cross-cultural · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Honne (true feelings, genuine thoughts) and tatemae (public facade, socially expected behavior) represent the Japanese recognition that humans always operate with two distinct layers of expression. Tatemae is not hypocrisy but social lubrication -- the practiced surface that maintains group harmony (wa), respects hierarchy, and prevents the damage of unfiltered honesty. Honne is reserved for trusted intimates or expressed through indirect channels (drinking sessions, anonymous feedback, trusted intermediaries). While Westerners often view this duality as dishonesty, Japanese culture sees it as maturity and social responsibility. The skill lies not in eliminating the gap but in reading it -- understanding what people really mean through context, tone, timing, and what is not said. Every culture has honne/tatemae dynamics; Japan simply names and acknowledges them explicitly.
- **How it helps:** Understanding honne and tatemae dramatically improves cross-cultural communication and organizational intelligence. It explains why direct feedback cultures (US, Netherlands) and indirect feedback cultures (Japan, Korea) consistently misunderstand each other. In any organization, recognizing the gap between what people say in meetings (tatemae) and what they actually think (honne) is critical intelligence. Leaders who create channels for honne expression (anonymous surveys, informal settings, trusted intermediaries) make better decisions than those who take tatemae at face value. The model also provides self-awareness: understanding your own public/private gap improves authenticity.
- **When to use:** When interpreting communication in any cross-cultural or high-context organizational setting. When meeting responses seem uniformly positive but implementation resistance appears. When building feedback systems that need to capture genuine opinion. When negotiating and sensing that stated positions don't reflect actual interests.
- **Unique value:** Makes explicit what most cultures leave implicit: the permanent, functional gap between public expression and private belief. By naming and legitimizing this gap, it prevents naive literalism in interpretation and provides a framework for reading between the lines systematically.
- **Limits:** Can be used to dismiss all disagreement as 'just tatemae' and project desired interpretations onto others' silence. Overemphasis on reading hidden meanings can lead to paranoid interpretation. In low-context cultures, genuine directness may be misread as tatemae. The concept can excuse manipulative dishonesty.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Japanese cultural psychology (Doi), developmental psychology of attachment, and relational psychoanalysis. Connects to object relations theory (Winnicott), Bowlby's attachment theory, and cross-cultural psychology (Hofstede's individualism-collectivism dimension). Draws on sociology of dependency and interdependence in social relationships.

### Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions · #1958 · `llm-high` · cross-cultural · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Geert Hofstede's framework identifies six dimensions along which national cultures systematically vary: Power Distance (acceptance of unequal power), Individualism vs. Collectivism, Masculinity vs. Femininity (competition vs. cooperation), Uncertainty Avoidance (tolerance for ambiguity), Long-Term vs. Short-Term Orientation, and Indulgence vs. Restraint. Derived from a landmark IBM survey of 100,000+ employees across 70+ countries, later expanded with World Values Survey data. Each dimension is scored 0-100 per country, enabling quantitative cross-cultural comparison. The model reveals that behaviors often attributed to personality are frequently cultural programming operating at the collective level.
- **How it helps:** Transforms vague intuitions about cultural difference into measurable dimensions. Before a negotiation with Japanese partners, checking their high Uncertainty Avoidance score (92) explains the emphasis on detailed contracts. When an American startup expands to Sweden, the low Masculinity score (5) predicts aggressive sales tactics will backfire. HR teams use it to design expatriate training: an employee moving from low Power Distance Denmark to high Power Distance Malaysia needs coaching on hierarchical communication. Prevents costly misattributions -- what looks like indecisiveness in a high Uncertainty Avoidance culture is thoroughness.
- **When to use:** When designing products, negotiations, management practices, or communications for multiple national cultures. Essential for multinational team management, international marketing, and expatriate preparation. Use when cultural friction emerges and you need a diagnostic framework rather than stereotyping.
- **Unique value:** Only framework offering quantitative, empirically derived scores across 100+ countries on six validated dimensions. Moves cultural analysis from anecdote to data. Its massive sample size and replication across decades gives predictive power that narrative-based cultural models cannot match.
- **Limits:** Treats nations as culturally homogeneous, ignoring regional, ethnic, generational, and class variation. Scores are averages masking enormous within-country diversity. The original IBM data reflects corporate employees, not whole populations. Dimensions can reify stereotypes if used rigidly. Critics note cultures shift over time, but scores update slowly.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in cross-cultural psychology (Hofstede, Triandis) and empirical organizational sociology. Draws on IBM employee survey data across 70+ countries and subsequent World Values Survey validation. Connects to evolutionary psychology of in-group/out-group dynamics, cultural learning theory, and anthropology of normative systems.
