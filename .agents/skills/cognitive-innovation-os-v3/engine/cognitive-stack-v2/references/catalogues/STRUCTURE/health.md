### Therapeutic Window (Too Little vs Too Much) · #529 · `high` · health · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The pharmacological concept that every treatment has a range where it's effective—below the window, the dose is too low to help; above it, the dose becomes toxic. The therapeutic window captures the critical insight that MORE is not always better and that the same substance that heals at one dose kills at another. Applied broadly: every intervention—management attention, regulation, investment, communication—has a therapeutic window. Too little management is neglect; optimal management is productive; too much management is micromanagement. The dose defines whether it's medicine or poison.
- **How it helps:** For any input you're considering increasing, ask: am I in the therapeutic window, approaching its upper bound, or already past it? The symptoms of being above the therapeutic window are paradoxical—the same input that helped when you started now seems to be making things worse. More meetings don't produce more alignment; they produce meeting fatigue. More features don't produce more satisfaction; they produce complexity. When increasing an input starts producing worse outcomes, you've exceeded the therapeutic window and need to reduce, not increase.
- **When to use:** When more effort or resources are producing worse outcomes (past the upper bound); when interventions seem to have no effect (below the lower bound); when calibrating the right amount of any organizational input—oversight, communication, process, support; when designing dosing strategies for any intervention.
- **Unique value:** Applies pharmacological precision to the vague advice 'find the right balance.' Instead of 'don't micromanage but don't neglect,' the therapeutic window says: there's a specific range where management attention is helpful, and the boundaries are identifiable through outcome monitoring. This makes 'balance' measurable.
- **Limits:** The therapeutic window varies by individual, context, and over time. Finding the window requires experimentation and monitoring. In complex systems, multiple inputs interact, making it hard to isolate any single input's window. The metaphor from pharmacology to management is imperfect—organizational 'doses' are harder to measure than drug doses.
- **Foundations:** Founded in pharmacology (therapeutic index), toxicology, and clinical pharmacokinetics. Connects to systems biology (homeostatic set points), hormesis research, and optimal control theory in engineering. Therapeutic windows exist across biological, psychological, and organizational systems wherever dose-response curves are nonlinear.

### Evidence Hierarchy (EBM Pyramid) · #2012 · `high` · health · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Evidence-based medicine organizes evidence into a hierarchy from weakest to strongest: expert opinion and case reports at the bottom, case-control studies above them, cohort studies higher, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) near the top, and systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs at the apex. Each level up provides more protection against bias: case reports can't control for confounders, observational studies are vulnerable to selection bias, RCTs control for both but may lack generalizability, and systematic reviews synthesize multiple RCTs to increase power and reduce the influence of any single flawed study. The practitioner's nuance: the hierarchy is a guideline, not a straitjacket. A well-designed cohort study may provide better evidence than a poorly designed RCT. Some questions (rare diseases, surgical techniques, long-term outcomes) can never be studied with RCTs.
- **How it helps:** This hierarchy provides a universal framework for evaluating the quality of evidence in any domain. When someone cites 'research shows...', you can ask: what kind of research? A single case study, a large randomized trial, or a meta-analysis? In business, most 'evidence' for management practices is at the expert opinion or case study level — useful but far weaker than people treat it. In policy debates, understanding the evidence hierarchy helps you distinguish between 'we have a randomized trial showing this works' and 'a few successful companies did this.' The hierarchy doesn't tell you what to believe, but it calibrates how confident you should be.
- **When to use:** When evaluating claims backed by 'evidence,' designing research or data-gathering approaches, deciding how much confidence to place in a finding, arguing for or against a proposed intervention based on the quality of supporting evidence, or distinguishing strong evidence from compelling anecdotes.
- **Unique value:** Provides an explicit, well-tested ranking system for evidence quality that transfers across domains. The practitioner additions — knowing when the hierarchy should be violated and understanding the distinction between internal and external validity — prevent rigid misapplication.
- **Limits:** The hierarchy can be used to dismiss all non-RCT evidence, which is impractical for most real-world decisions. It addresses risk of bias but not relevance (a perfect RCT studying the wrong question is useless). Publication bias can corrupt even meta-analyses. Some domains (education, management) may never generate RCT-level evidence for their most important questions.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in clinical epidemiology (Guyatt, Sackett), philosophy of science (hierarchy of evidence quality), and medical research methodology. Connects to systematic review methodology (Cochrane Collaboration), meta-analytic statistics, and research design theory. Draws on Bradford Hill's causal criteria and evidence synthesis scholarship.
